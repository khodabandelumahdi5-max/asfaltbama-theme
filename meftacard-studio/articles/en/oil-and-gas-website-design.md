# Oil and Gas Website Design: What Energy Buyers Check First

An oil and gas company's website is rarely where a contract is signed. It is where the shortlist is made. A procurement engineer checking prequalification, an investor reading the HSE record, a regulator confirming certifications, a candidate looking for field roles: each arrives with a specific question and leaves in under a minute if the site cannot answer it.

That makes oil and gas website design less about visual style and more about **evidence, structure and speed**. This guide covers what those visitors look for and how to build a site that answers them.

## Design for four audiences, not one

Most energy websites fail because they are written for "everyone". In practice there are four distinct visitors:

| Visitor | What they need in the first 30 seconds |
|---|---|
| Procurement and engineering | Capabilities, certifications, equipment, project references, how to register as a supplier or request a quote |
| Investors and partners | Company overview, leadership, financial or annual reports, ESG and HSE performance |
| Regulators and government | Licences, compliance statements, HSE policies, local-content commitments |
| Talent | Careers, locations, field versus office roles, how to apply |

Give each audience a clear entry point from the home page and the main navigation. A common and effective pattern is to segment services by **upstream, midstream and downstream**, so a buyer can find their part of the value chain immediately.

## The evidence that builds trust

Energy buyers are trained sceptics. Adjectives like "world-class" carry no weight. What does:

- **Certifications with numbers and dates.** ISO 9001, 14001, 45001, API monogram licences, with scope and validity, ideally downloadable.
- **Project references.** Client (where permitted), location, scope, duration and outcome. One detailed case study beats ten logos.
- **HSE performance.** Policies, and figures where you publish them. An HSE section that is only a stock photo of a hard hat signals the opposite of safety.
- **Technical documentation.** Datasheets, capability statements and brochures as properly named PDFs, also summarised in HTML so search engines can read them.
- **People.** Named leadership and, for technical services, named engineering leads.

## Structure that procurement teams expect

A practical sitemap for a mid-sized energy services company:

1. Home
2. About (history, leadership, locations, local content)
3. Capabilities, segmented by value chain or service line
4. Projects and case studies
5. HSE and quality
6. Investors or corporate reports (where relevant)
7. Suppliers and tenders (registration, policies, open tenders)
8. Careers
9. News and insights
10. Contact, with separate routes for sales, procurement and media

The **supplier and tender portal** is often the most valuable page on the site and the most neglected. Clear registration steps and contact details reduce wasted emails for both sides.

## Bilingual by design in the GCC

In the Gulf, government stakeholders and many local partners prefer Arabic, while international contractors and investors work in English. Treat both as first-class:

- Separate URLs for each language, with hreflang so Google shows the right version.
- Arabic typography and right-to-left layout designed in, not flipped automatically.
- Equivalent depth. An Arabic section with half the content tells Arabic-speaking stakeholders they are second priority.

## Performance is a credibility signal

Field engineers open your site on a phone, on site, over a weak connection. A five-second load reads as an outdated company. Set measurable targets and hold the build to them:

- Largest Contentful Paint under about 2.5 seconds on mobile (we target under 1.8).
- Compressed images and video, no autoplay hero videos on mobile.
- Minimal third-party scripts; every tag manager snippet has a cost.

## Security and sovereignty

Energy companies are high-value targets. The website should be treated as part of the security perimeter:

- Managed hosting with a web application firewall, backups and monitoring.
- Hosting in-region where policy or regulation requires it.
- Code, domains and accounts owned by the company, not the agency.
- Documented access control: who can publish, who can change DNS.

## Capturing qualified leads without looking salesy

Industrial buyers do not want a pop-up asking for their email. They do want:

- A short **request-for-quote form** on every capability page, routed to the right team.
- **Direct contact options** that match how the region works: phone, email and WhatsApp or Telegram for quick questions.
- A downloadable capability statement in exchange for minimal details.

## A checklist before you brief an agency

- [ ] Which four audiences matter most, in order?
- [ ] Which certifications, references and documents can be published?
- [ ] Arabic, English or both, with equal depth?
- [ ] Who needs a portal (suppliers, dealers, investors)?
- [ ] Performance and uptime targets in the contract?
- [ ] Who owns the code, domain and hosting?
