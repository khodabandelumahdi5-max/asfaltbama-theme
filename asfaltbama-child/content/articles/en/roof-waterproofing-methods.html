Roof waterproofing is a building's most important defence against water. A flat roof in Iraq or the Gulf takes direct sun all day, cools sharply at night, bakes at extreme temperatures in summer and then faces short but intense downpours in winter. Any weakness in its waterproofing sooner or later shows up as ceiling stains, blistered plaster, corroded reinforcement and damage to the top-floor interior. Today there is a wide range of roof waterproofing methods: bituminous membranes such as Isogam and built-up hot bitumen and jute, liquid-applied polymer and bituminous coatings, cementitious coatings, synthetic sheets such as PVC and TPO, and sprayed polyurethane foam. Each has its strengths, limitations and ideal conditions.

This guide introduces every common roof waterproofing method, compares them on durability, cost, speed, sun and traffic resistance and suitability for different roofs, and gives a clear recommendation for each type of roof, from exposed residential roofs to tiled, sloping, industrial and green roofs. It then covers thermal insulation and the inverted roof, layer sequences, special details, common mistakes and a maintenance plan.

Key points at a glance

  • For most exposed residential roofs, a foil-faced APP membrane installed properly is the common, sensible choice.
  • Under tiles and screeds, bitumen and jute or a plain membrane; on very busy surfaces, bitumen and jute or a reinforced liquid system.
  • Liquid systems suit repairs, complex details and sites where hot works are banned, but their quality depends on the dry film thickness and preparation.
  • No waterproofing replaces correct falls, enough drains and careful details.
  • Waterproofing and thermal insulation are different things, and most Gulf roofs need both, often as an inverted roof.

Why roof waterproofing matters so much

Water entering through a roof is not just a stain on the ceiling. Persistent moisture leads to:

  • Corrosion of reinforcement and steel: moisture rusts the reinforcement in slabs and ribs; rust expands and cracks the surrounding concrete. In coastal cities, chlorides in the air make this faster.
  • Damage to finishes: blistered plaster, peeling paint and mould, especially on the top floor.
  • Risk to electrical services: water in ceiling conduits and light fittings creates short-circuit and safety risks.
  • Loss of thermal performance: wet insulation loses much of its value, increasing cooling costs in a region where air conditioning already dominates energy bills.
  • Disputes: in apartment buildings, roof leaks often lead to disputes between the top-floor owner and building management.

The cost of a sound waterproofing job is usually a small fraction of the cost of repairing this damage. Roof waterproofing should be seen as an investment, not a cost to be minimised with the cheapest offer.

Warning signs that a roof needs re-waterproofing

  • Deep, networked cracks on exposed bitumen or unfaced membranes.
  • Foil peeling off large areas of the membrane with cracked bitumen underneath.
  • Large or growing blisters, or areas that sound hollow underfoot.
  • Upstands lifting or separating from the parapet.
  • Reinforcement fibres exposed on the surface and crumbling.
  • Water standing on the roof for days after rain.
  • More and more patches, with a new leak every season.
  • Stains or blistered plaster on the top-floor ceiling, however small.

Early diagnosis usually means a local repair instead of full replacement, and prevents damage to the slab and the interior.

Survey and diagnosis before choosing a method

Before deciding on a type of waterproofing, you need to know exactly what condition the roof is in. A proper survey records:

The existing waterproofing

Is there any? Which type, how many layers? Is it sound and bonded, or cracked, blistered, rotten and delaminated? Pressing and tapping reveals hollow, debonded areas. On many older buildings there are several old layers beneath the visible one.

Falls and ponding

Dried water marks, silt deposits and algae map the low spots. If possible, pour a few buckets of water and watch where it runs. A roof where water stands needs its falls corrected before any waterproofing.

Drains and the drainage route

The number, size and condition of drains, and whether the downpipe is sound along its length. Sometimes a "roof leak" is actually a cracked downpipe inside a wall.

Details and equipment

AC units, ducts, water tanks, solar panels, antennas, gas pipes and vents. For each, decide whether it will be moved, how it is supported and how the waterproofing will be detailed around it.

Parapets and facades

Cracks in parapet render, open joints in stone or block at the top of the wall and missing copings can all bring water behind the waterproofing. A survey that ignores the walls is incomplete.

Leak locations below

Where the stains are, when they appear (immediately after rain or later, only in heavy rain or even in light rain) and how they change over time are important clues to the water's path.

Before the method: where good waterproofing starts

  1. A sound, dry substrateWaterproofing does not last on a wet, rotten, dusty or uneven substrate. Failed old layers must be removed and the surface repaired.
  2. Falls and enough drainsThe roof must carry water to the drains at about 1.5–2 percent, and the number and size of drains must cope with intense downpours. Ponding is the enemy of every waterproofing.
  3. Careful detailsFillets in corners, upstands with protected top edges, and carefully sealed drains and pipes. Most leaks come from details.

If these three foundations are right, almost any suitable waterproofing will perform well. If they are not, even the most expensive system will leak.

1. Modified bitumen membranes (Isogam)

Prefabricated rolls of polymer-modified bitumen (APP or SBS) with a central reinforcement, torched onto a primed surface. The most widely used roof waterproofing in Iran and common across Iraq and the Gulf.

Advantages

  • Uniform factory quality with verifiable data on each roll.
  • Fast installation and a relatively clean site.
  • Foil-faced membranes resist sun and can stay exposed.
  • A wide working temperature range thanks to modified bitumen.

Limitations

  • Laps between rolls; their quality depends on installation.
  • Many cuts and patches on very busy surfaces.
  • Torch-applied; restricted on some sites.
  • Needs protection against heavy traffic and mechanical damage.

Best for: most exposed residential and commercial roofs, large roofs as a two-layer system, and under finishes (plain membranes). See our Isogam roof membrane installation guide.

2. Hot bitumen and jute (built-up)

A built-in-place system of alternating hot bitumen and jute, usually two layers of jute between three coats of bitumen.

Advantages

  • Continuous, seamless and fully bonded.
  • Excellent on irregular surfaces and details.
  • Very good under tiles, in foundations and wet areas.
  • Simple repairs with the same materials.

Limitations

  • Quality depends entirely on the crew.
  • Cracks early when exposed to sun; protection essential.
  • Hot bitumen, fumes and burn risk.
  • Slower than a membrane.

Best for: under tiles and screeds, foundations and basements, wet areas and busy surfaces. See our bitumen and jute roofing guide and the comparison of the two systems.

3. Liquid-applied waterproofing

Liquid materials applied by brush, roller or spray that cure into a continuous, seamless skin. The main families:

  • Polyurethane: high elasticity and adhesion; some grades need a UV-stable topcoat.
  • Acrylic (water-based): easy to apply without strong odour, good sun resistance, usually light-coloured and reflective; weaker under prolonged ponding.
  • Bitumen-rubber or bitumen-polymer: good flexibility; mostly for buried applications and foundations.
  • Polyurea and other spray systems: machine-applied, very fast curing and mechanically tough; require specialised equipment and operators.

Advantages

  • Seamless; forms easily around pipes, plinths and complex details.
  • No flame; suitable where hot works are banned.
  • Lightweight; suits weight-sensitive roofs.
  • Good for repairs and for overcoating sound old waterproofing.

Limitations

  • Final thickness depends on the applicator; thin films fail early.
  • Very sensitive to preparation and substrate moisture.
  • Some grades are weak under prolonged ponding.
  • Product quality on the market varies widely.

The key to a liquid system is the dry film thickness: consumption per square metre and the number of coats must follow the manufacturer and be written into the contract. A reinforcing fabric is normally embedded at corners and details, and in the Gulf heat, application windows and recoat times need particular care.

4. Cementitious waterproofing

Polymer-modified cementitious slurries or mortars brushed or trowelled onto concrete, in rigid or flexible grades.

  • Advantages: excellent bond to concrete, tolerance of damp substrates (some grades), tiles can be laid directly on top, no flame.
  • Limitations: limited crack-bridging, especially rigid grades; not usually the first choice for exposed roofs with large temperature swings.

Best for: water tanks, swimming pools, wet areas under tiles, some basement applications from the inside, and low-movement concrete that will be tiled directly.

5. Synthetic sheets (PVC, TPO and EPDM)

Prefabricated plastic or synthetic rubber membranes in wide rolls. PVC and TPO seams are hot-air welded; EPDM is joined with adhesives and tapes. Widely used on large commercial and industrial roofs worldwide and increasingly on Gulf warehouses and logistics buildings.

  • Advantages: light, wide rolls with fewer seams, good UV resistance (depending on type), mechanically fixed or ballasted without full bonding.
  • Limitations: need welding equipment and trained installers; repairs require compatible materials; limited compatibility with bitumen (PVC must not be in direct contact with bitumen).

Best for: large industrial roofs, warehouses, lightweight roofs and projects where the designer specifies them.

6. Sprayed polyurethane foam

Sprayed polyurethane foam, a familiar sight on roofs in Kuwait, Saudi Arabia and the UAE, is both thermal insulation and a waterproof layer: the foam is sprayed, expands and hardens into a continuous layer, which is then covered with a UV-protective coating. It is used on concrete roofs, metal roofs and irregular surfaces. Its quality depends heavily on the equipment, application temperature, substrate and operator skill, and the protective topcoat must be maintained and renewed periodically, because foam degrades quickly once exposed to sun.

Combined systems

On many roofs the best result comes from combining methods: a membrane on the field with bitumen and jute or a reinforced liquid system at complex details; a bitumen and jute base with a foil-faced membrane cap sheet on sensitive roofs; or a liquid coating as a protective overcoat on sound old membranes. The conditions for success are material compatibility (for example, a liquid product that is compatible with a bituminous surface) and careful execution at the junction between systems. Get this information from the manufacturers and write it into the specification.

Choosing by type of roof

Exposed residential roof

For most roofs with no finish on top, a foil-faced APP membrane with polyester reinforcement is the usual choice. Where the roof has many pipes and plinths, details are reinforced with bitumen and jute or a reinforced liquid system.

Tiled roofs and roofs with a screed

Two-layer bitumen and jute or a plain membrane, with a separation layer between waterproofing and mortar and a flood test before tiling. Movement joints in the tiling stop thermal movement of the tiles from stressing the waterproofing.

Terraces and balconies

Bitumen and jute, a plain membrane or a reinforced liquid system; the critical point is the door threshold, where the waterproofing must pass under the frame and turn up the wall.

Sloping concrete roofs

A modified bitumen membrane that does not creep in heat, or a liquid system suitable for slopes, usually under a final covering such as tiles or sheeting.

Industrial roofs and warehouses

A two-layer membrane, a synthetic sheet, or for metal roofs, sprayed foam with a protective coating or dedicated sheet-roof systems. Phasing the work around operations is critical; these projects are often planned together with asphalt paving of yards and access roads.

Roofs with heavy traffic and podium decks

The waterproofing must sit under a robust protective layer: tiles, protective concrete or asphalt. On basement car park roofs that are paved with asphalt, the waterproofing must withstand hot asphalt and roller loads, which needs careful layer design and coordination with the paving crew.

Green roofs and planters

The waterproofing must be fully continuous and root-resistant, or protected by a root barrier, with proper drainage. Ordinary waterproofing without root protection is not enough.

Older roofs with several layers

Before choosing a method, decide what happens to the old layers. Stacked layers add weight and trap moisture; in most cases stripping them and starting on a sound substrate is the more economical long-term choice.

Metal roofs, warehouses and lightweight roofs

Metal sheet roofs work differently. Leaks typically come from fixings, side and end laps, internal gutters and rooflights. Solutions depend on the roof's condition: replacing fasteners and washers, sealing laps with dedicated tapes and sealants, applying a reinforced liquid system over laps or the whole surface, or sprayed foam with a protective coating. Torching a bitumen membrane onto thin metal sheet is rarely appropriate because of the sheet's large thermal movement and poor bond, unless a dedicated substrate is installed. On lightweight roofs, the weight of waterproofing and protective layers must be checked against the structure.

Weight and roof loading

Each method adds a different weight to the roof, which matters on older buildings, lightweight roofs and roofs that have been waterproofed several times. Liquid systems and synthetic sheets are lightest, membranes are in the middle and multi-layer bitumen and jute is heavier. But most of the weight usually comes from the associated layers: lightweight screed to falls, mortar, tiles, or the gravel on an inverted roof. When refurbishing older roofs, removing redundant old layers makes the waterproofing more reliable and reduces the load. Where heavy layers such as tiles or gravel are added, a structural check is recommended.

New construction versus refurbishment

On new buildings, roof waterproofing can be designed properly from the start: falls formed in the screed, enough drains in the right places, kerbs for ducts and plinths for plant built before waterproofing, and the insulation arrangement chosen with the structure in mind. The main risks on new projects are programme pressure, where waterproofing is rushed onto damp screeds, and follow-on trades who damage the finished waterproofing while installing services. Protecting the roof and controlling access after waterproofing are as important as the installation itself.

On existing buildings, the roof has to be understood before it can be fixed: old layers, hidden damage, poor falls, undersized drains and equipment installed over the years. Refurbishment usually involves more preparation than new work, and the programme must allow for it. It is also the moment to correct design faults, such as adding drains, raising kerbs or relocating plant, rather than simply laying new waterproofing over old problems.

Designing for the climate of Iraq and the Gulf

  • Extreme heat and UV: surface temperatures on dark roofs become extreme in summer. Reflective finishes, foil-faced membranes and buried systems under tiles or in inverted roofs perform best; exposed bitumen and many coatings age quickly.
  • Large daily temperature swings: movement at corners and joints is constant; fillets, movement joints and flexible materials matter.
  • Dust and sand: dust storms contaminate surfaces during work and block drains afterwards. Clean immediately before priming, stop work in storms and clean drains regularly.
  • Short, intense rain: drains must be sized for downpours, not for average rainfall, and kept clear.
  • Coastal humidity and salt: dew delays drying, and chlorides attack reinforcement; waiting for a dry substrate and protecting the slab from water are doubly important.
  • Colder mountain areas: in parts of the Kurdistan Region, frost and snow call for cold-flexible (SBS) materials and higher upstands.

Thermal insulation and the inverted roof

Waterproofing keeps out water, not heat. In the Gulf, the roof is the main source of heat gain in low-rise buildings and top-floor apartments, and building codes in several Gulf states set minimum roof insulation levels. There are two main ways to combine insulation and waterproofing:

Conventional roof (waterproofing on top)

Insulation on the slab, then the screed to falls and the waterproofing on top. The waterproofing is exposed to the sun and must be protected, and a vapour control layer may be needed under the insulation to prevent condensation within it.

Inverted roof (waterproofing below)

Waterproofing on the slab after the falls, then extruded polystyrene boards that do not absorb water, a filter layer and ballast such as gravel or pavers. The waterproofing is protected from sun, impact and temperature swings and lasts much longer, but the flood test must be done before the boards go down. Very common on Gulf projects.

Lightweight screed provides some insulation but rarely replaces real insulation boards. The arrangement and thickness of insulation are set by the designer in line with local regulations.

Special details that matter with every method

Stair enclosures and roof doors

The walls of stair enclosures need upstands and fillets like parapets, and the roof door threshold, where the waterproofing must pass under the frame, is a common leak point. The enclosure roof must also be waterproofed and drained to a gutter, not onto its own walls.

AC ducts and openings

Ducts passing through the roof must sit on a raised kerb with the waterproofing turned up it. A duct opening flush with the roof, without a kerb and flashing, almost always leaks.

Light wells and courtyards

The roof edge beside a light well must be detailed so water does not spill into it, with the waterproofing turned over the edge and protected by a drip or capping.

Roofs without parapets

The waterproofing runs over the edge, down the fascia and is finished with a metal drip edge so water falls clear of the building.

Junctions with neighbouring buildings

The gap between adjoining buildings collects water. A metal cover or flexible detail over the separation joint, coordinated with the neighbour, prevents leaks there.

Expansion joints

Waterproofing must not simply run tight across an expansion joint. A flexible loop or separate strip lets the structure move without tearing it.

Water tanks, solar panels and rooftop plant

Rooftop water tanks are almost universal on residential buildings in Iraq and common across the region, and solar panels are spreading quickly. Both are among the most frequent causes of roof leaks. Tanks and heavy plant must stand on plinths built before waterproofing, with the waterproofing turned up around them; overflows and plant drainage must be piped to drains. Solar mounts should preferably be ballasted on protected blocks; where they must be fixed to the slab, each fixing needs its own flashing. Above all, waterproof first and install equipment afterwards in coordination with the waterproofing contractor, rather than drilling through a new roof.

Apartment buildings: coordinating the owners

In multi-unit buildings the roof is common property, and decisions about waterproofing usually involve the owners' association or building management. Practical advice: obtain a technical survey and a written proposal with a clear scope before collecting contributions, so everyone knows what they are paying for; inform residents of the programme, especially if hot bitumen will cause fumes or AC units must be moved temporarily; require owners of AC units and antennas not to drill or fix anything on the roof afterwards without coordination; and keep the handover record and warranty in the building file for future managers.

Specifying roof waterproofing in tender documents

Many waterproofing disputes begin with a vague line such as "waterproofing to roof". A good specification states at least:

  • The system and its layers, including insulation and protection where applicable.
  • The products: type, grade, thickness, reinforcement, finish and manufacturer.
  • Preparation: stripping, repairs, falls, fillets and primer.
  • Details, preferably drawn: upstands and terminations, drains, penetrations, expansion joints and edges.
  • Testing: flood test duration, witness and record.
  • Measurement rules: how upstands, laps and details are measured.
  • Warranty: scope, duration and conditions.

With this, quotations become comparable and supervisors have clear acceptance criteria.

Estimating the area

The waterproofing area is always larger than the roof area on the drawings. Add the plan area, the upstands on all parapets and stair enclosures (length × upstand height), the kerbs and plinths, and the enclosure roof. Then add an allowance for laps and waste depending on the method. On busy roofs, the difference between the real area and the drawing area can be significant, so check whether a price quoted per "square metre of roof" includes upstands and details.

What affects the cost?

  • Method and materials: type, brand, thickness and number of layers.
  • Preparation: stripping, disposal, repairs, falls and fillets.
  • Details: drains, pipes, plinths, parapet lengths and expansion joints.
  • Access: building height, lifting materials and working-hour restrictions.
  • Protection and insulation: coatings, tiles, gravel, insulation boards.
  • Area: on small roofs, mobilisation takes a larger share.

For a fair comparison, ask contractors to define the scope precisely: preparation, product data, upstand height, details, flood test, protection and warranty.

Safety by method

  • Membranes: open flame on the roof; cylinders in the shade and away from the flame, extinguisher ready, combustibles and duct openings protected; hot work permits where required.
  • Bitumen and jute: hot bitumen and severe burn risk; protective clothing, stable boiler, never water on hot bitumen.
  • Solvent-based liquids: flammable, irritating vapours; ventilation, no flames, suitable respirators.
  • Spray systems: high-pressure equipment and reactive chemicals; trained operators with full PPE only.
  • All methods: fall protection at open edges, safe lifting of materials, and heat-stress precautions for crews in the Gulf summer.

What determines how long a roof lasts?

Clients often ask how many years a waterproofing will last. The honest answer is that there is no fixed number; it depends on material quality, workmanship, falls and ponding, sun protection, traffic and mechanical damage, climate and maintenance. Two roofs with the same product on the same street can perform very differently. Rather than chasing general figures, focus on what is in your control: the right choice, proper installation, testing, protection and maintenance, and ask for a written warranty with a defined scope for each project.

Ten common mistakes

  1. Choosing waterproofing by price alone, without considering how the roof is used.
  2. Laying new waterproofing over rotten, wet old layers.
  3. Ignoring falls and ponding.
  4. Omitting fillets and primer to save money.
  5. Short upstands with loose top edges.
  6. Careless detailing at drains and pipes.
  7. Liquid systems applied thinner than specified.
  8. Leaving bitumen and jute or sun-sensitive systems exposed.
  9. Tiling or ballasting without a flood test.
  10. Installing equipment after waterproofing without coordination and puncturing it.

Maintenance

  • Inspect the roof twice a year and after every major dust storm.
  • Clear drains and gratings of sand, leaves and plastic.
  • Sweep off sand and silt so plants cannot take root.
  • Check upstand edges and laps and repair any lifting promptly.
  • Renew protective coatings on unfaced membranes, bitumen and foam at the intervals set by the manufacturer.
  • Brief AC and service technicians not to leave sharp tools on the roof or drill without coordination.
  • In apartment buildings, keep the handover record and warranty in the building file.

Have a leak? Diagnose before replacing

If the roof leaks, find the cause before choosing a new method. Many leaks come from blocked drains, open upstands, mechanical damage or cracks in the parapet render, not from the waterproofing itself. Replacing the waterproofing without removing the cause will not solve the problem.

When to waterproof

In most of Iraq and the Gulf, the best periods are the cooler, dry months of autumn and spring, when substrates dry quickly and extreme heat does not complicate installation. Work is possible in summer, but crews start early, stop in the midday heat, store materials in the shade and keep traffic off fresh work. In winter, avoid rainy days and wait until the substrate is fully dry; on the coast, wait for morning dew to evaporate.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best roof waterproofing?

The best waterproofing is the one that suits how your roof is used and is installed properly. For most exposed residential roofs, a foil-faced APP membrane; under tiles, bitumen and jute or a plain membrane; for busy details and repairs, a reinforced liquid system or bitumen and jute; for insulated Gulf roofs, often an inverted roof or sprayed foam with a protective coating.

Is a liquid system better than a membrane?

Each has its place. Liquid systems are seamless and excellent at complex details, but their quality depends heavily on film thickness and preparation. Membranes offer uniform factory quality and are more common on exposed roofs.

Can a liquid coating go over an old membrane?

On a sound, clean, bonded membrane, some liquid systems can be applied with a suitable primer. Rotten, blistered or debonded membranes must be removed or repaired first. Check compatibility with the manufacturer.

Is cementitious waterproofing suitable for roofs?

For exposed roofs with large temperature swings and possible cracking, it is not usually the first choice. It performs better in tanks, pools, under tiles and on low-movement concrete.

Is sprayed foam a good choice?

It combines insulation and waterproofing and is widely used in the Gulf, but it depends heavily on application quality, and its protective coating must be maintained, because exposed foam degrades quickly in the sun.

Do I need thermal insulation as well?

In the Gulf climate, almost certainly. Roof insulation significantly reduces cooling loads, and waterproofing does not replace it.

How long does roof waterproofing take?

For a typical residential roof, the waterproofing itself takes one to several days; preparation, drying of screeds and primer, and the flood test add to that.

Can I waterproof over old tiles?

Torching a membrane directly onto old tiles is not usually recommended. Some liquid systems can be applied over sound tiles with proper preparation, but the falls and the condition of the waterproofing beneath the tiles must be checked.

Should the roof be waterproofed before or after installing AC units and tanks?

Before. Build plinths and kerbs first, waterproof the roof including the plinths, and then install equipment in coordination with the waterproofing contractor. Drilling through a finished roof is one of the most common causes of new leaks.

Can waterproofing be done while the building is occupied?

Yes. Work is planned in sections so the roof is never left open overnight, fumes are managed away from windows and AC intakes, and cold-applied systems can be chosen where hot works are not acceptable.

Conclusion

Roof waterproofing does not start with choosing "the best material"; it starts with understanding the roof: its use, its finish, its details, the old waterproofing, the falls and the climate. On that basis, membranes, bitumen and jute, liquid systems, cementitious coatings, synthetic sheets and sprayed foam each have their place. With every method, proper preparation, careful details, a flood test and regular maintenance make the difference between a roof that works for years and one that leaks in its first rainy season.
