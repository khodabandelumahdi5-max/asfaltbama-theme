Isogam membrane or bitumen and jute? Almost every owner, developer or project manager planning to waterproof a roof, terrace, bathroom or foundation in Iraq or the Gulf faces this question, and usually hears two contradictory answers: one person says bitumen and jute is outdated, another says nothing beats it. The truth is that both methods are bitumen-based, both make a reliable waterproofing when properly installed, and each one outperforms the other in certain conditions. The right choice depends on where the waterproofing goes, how the surface will be used, what finish goes on top, the shape of the surface, the climate and the budget.

In this guide we compare an Isogam (modified bitumen) membrane with hot bitumen and jute roofing across twelve criteria, give a clear recommendation for each location (exposed roof, tiled roof, terrace, bathroom, foundation and basement wall, industrial roof), look at common myths, and finish with a simple decision table so you can make an informed choice for your own building.

Key points at a glance

  • Exposed residential roof: a foil-faced Isogam membrane is usually the more sensible choice; faster, more uniform and more resistant to the sun.
  • Under tiles, in foundations and wet areas: bitumen and jute is an excellent option thanks to its full bond and flexibility; plain membranes are also used.
  • Busy, irregular surfaces: bitumen and jute has the upper hand.
  • Large and industrial roofs: a two-layer membrane system or a combination of both methods.
  • In both methods, workmanship matters more than the type of waterproofing.

The two systems at a glance

Hot bitumen and jute

A built-in-place waterproofing made of alternating layers of hot bitumen and jute (hessian); the usual system is two layers of jute between three coats of bitumen. The bitumen is melted on site and spread on the surface, and the jute is embedded in it. The result is a continuous skin fully bonded to the substrate. Our bitumen and jute roofing guide explains the full process.

Isogam membrane

A factory-made waterproofing supplied in rolls, made of polymer-modified bitumen with a central reinforcement (polyester, glass fibre or jute) and a foil, mineral or plain finish. On site, the underside of the roll is heated with a torch and bonded to the surface. Its thickness and composition are controlled at the factory. Our Isogam installation guide covers the installation step by step.

The fundamental difference can be summed up in one sentence: bitumen and jute is built on site, while a membrane is installed on site. Almost every advantage and limitation of each method follows from that difference.

Uniformity and quality control

The biggest advantage of a membrane is uniform factory quality. Thickness, bitumen formulation, reinforcement and finish are set on the production line, and the client can read the brand, type and thickness on the roll label and compare them with the contract. With bitumen and jute, all of this is decided on site by the crew: a skilled crew builds an excellent system, and a rushed crew delivers a poor one from the same materials. For a client who cannot supervise closely, this difference is significant.

Membranes are not immune to poor workmanship either: a good membrane without primer, with too little heat, narrow laps or poor detailing leaks just as badly. The difference is that part of the quality, the material itself, is guaranteed, and only the installation depends on the crew.

Bond and continuity

Hot bitumen is poured and spread, flowing into every irregularity, fine crack and corner, so almost no voids remain under a built-up system. That brings two benefits: if the system is damaged at one point, water cannot spread over a large area beneath it, and because the layers fuse together there are no true seams. With a membrane, the bond depends on the primer and the torching. A membrane bonded only in spots may let water travel underneath it after damage, making leaks harder to locate, and its laps are its sensitive points.

Adaptability to details

Many roofs are full of details: numerous pipes, AC plinths and antennas, ducts, water tanks, many corners, irregular walls and changes of level. A built-up system shapes itself easily to these surfaces. A membrane must be cut, patched and carefully torched at each of them; the more cuts, the more laps and the greater the chance of error.

Sun resistance

Bitumen in either system oxidises and becomes brittle under ultraviolet light. A foil-faced membrane carries a reflective aluminium face that protects the bitumen; exposed bitumen and jute has no protection at all. That is why an exposed bitumen and jute roof must always be protected with tiles, asphalt or a reflective coating, whereas a foil-faced membrane can be left exposed. In the Gulf and southern Iraq, where summer sun is extreme, this point is decisive.

Heat and cold

Membranes use polymer-modified bitumen with a wider working temperature range than ordinary bitumen: it softens later in heat and becomes brittle later in cold. A built-up system depends on the grade used: oxidised bitumen resists heat better, 60/70 is softer. On exposed roofs in very hot or cold regions, this difference matters more.

Weight

A two-layer bitumen and jute system is usually heavier than a single membrane, depending on how much bitumen is used. On most buildings this is not decisive, but on older or lightweight roofs, or where several layers of old waterproofing will remain, weight should be considered.

How each system resists six causes of deterioration

1. Sunlight

Exposed bitumen and jute suffers most from the sun, developing fine crazing until the jute shows through. A foil-faced membrane is protected while its foil is intact; when the foil wears, a reflective coating can take over.

2. Ponding water

Both suffer from ponding. In a built-up system the jute rots faster where water stands; in a membrane the laps are more exposed to water ingress under ponds. The cure is the same for both: correct falls and enough drains.

3. Temperature cycling

Roofs heat and cool every day, and the waterproofing expands and contracts with the structure. In the Gulf, daily swings are large. Modified bitumen copes better; in a built-up system, the bitumen grade and fillets at corners are decisive.

4. Mechanical damage

AC technicians, thin steel legs, sharp tools and careless maintenance damage both systems. Polyester-reinforced membranes resist tearing better; bitumen and jute is thicker but softens in heat. Busy roofs need protected walkways in either case.

5. Sand, silt and plants

Wind-blown sand and silt collect on roofs and hold moisture; seeds take root and roots can penetrate laps and cracks. Keeping the roof clean is essential for both systems. Planters and green roofs need root-resistant waterproofing, a separate topic.

6. Oils and chemicals

On industrial roofs and plant areas, spilled oil, diesel or solvents soften and dissolve bitumen. At these points both systems need a protective layer or drip trays.

Which system for which location?

Exposed residential roof

For most residential roofs with no finish on top, a foil-faced Isogam membrane is the usual and sensible choice: quick installation, uniform thickness, good sun resistance and verifiable product data. If the roof has many details, they can be reinforced with bitumen and jute while the field is covered with membrane.

Tiled roof

Flat roofs in Iraq and the Gulf are very often finished with tiles or pavers. Once the waterproofing is buried, the main weakness of bitumen and jute, sun sensitivity, disappears, and its strengths, full bond and no seams, come to the fore. Both methods are good choices here: plain membranes are popular for speed, bitumen and jute for continuity. In both cases, a flood test before tiling and a separation layer between waterproofing and mortar are essential.

Terraces and balconies

Terraces are usually tiled, small and full of details: door thresholds, floor drains, balustrades and walls. Either system works, but detailing the door threshold, where the waterproofing must pass under the frame and turn up the wall, matters more than the choice of system. Many terrace leaks start exactly there.

Bathrooms and wet areas

Bathrooms are small, full of pipes, floor drains and corners, and the waterproofing is always hidden under tiles. Bitumen and jute is very common thanks to its flexibility; plain membranes are also used with careful detailing. Turning the waterproofing up the walls, especially in the shower zone, sealing pipes and drains, and flood testing before tiling matter more than the system itself.

Foundations and basement walls

In permanent contact with soil and moisture, and often with saline groundwater in coastal areas and sabkha ground, the waterproofing must be fully continuous and will never be accessible again after backfilling. Multi-layer bitumen and jute or a suitable membrane, with protection against damage during concreting and backfilling, are both used. The choice depends on the groundwater level, the soil, the wall construction method and access.

Large, industrial and warehouse roofs

On large factory and warehouse roofs, speed and uniformity are important, so a two-layer membrane with staggered laps, or bitumen and jute at details with membrane on the field, is typically chosen. Coordination with operations and with other works on site also matters; these roofs are often planned together with asphalt paving of yards and access roads.

Sloping concrete roofs

On a slope, ordinary bitumen creeps downhill in heat. Modified bitumen membranes are more stable. If a built-up system is chosen, an oxidised bitumen with a high softening point must be used and a final covering laid over it.

Roofs with solar panels

Solar mounting creates many penetrations, and access to the waterproofing under the panels is difficult once they are installed. Waterproof before installing the panels and prefer ballasted mounts on protected concrete blocks that do not penetrate the waterproofing. Where mounts must be fixed to the slab, each one needs its own flashing, made with bitumen and jute or a membrane patch.

Roofs with tanks and heavy plant

Water tanks, a common sight on roofs across Iraq, and heavy plant should stand on plinths built before waterproofing, with the waterproofing turned up around them. Tank overflows and plant drainage must be piped to drains. Placing tanks directly on the waterproofing causes indentation and tears in either system.

Podium decks and slabs that will be paved with asphalt

Where asphalt is laid over the waterproofing, such as a basement car park roof that forms a yard above, the waterproofing must withstand hot asphalt and roller loads. Layer design, protection over the waterproofing and coordination with the paving crew are critical. We carry out both packages together so that the sequence and timing are right.

Choosing for the climate of Iraq and the Gulf

  • Extreme heat and sun (Basra, Kuwait, Bahrain, Dubai, Muscat): sun and heat resistance come first. Exposed bitumen and jute deteriorates quickly; a foil-faced APP membrane or a buried system under tiles or an inverted roof is preferred.
  • Coastal humidity: dew and humidity slow the drying of substrates and primer. In both methods, waiting for a completely dry substrate is doubly important.
  • Dust storms: sand settles on primer and hot bitumen during installation and blocks drains afterwards. Work stops during storms, and drains need regular cleaning.
  • Short, intense rain: annual rainfall is low but downpours can be heavy; drain capacity and correct falls are as important as the waterproofing.
  • Colder, mountainous areas (parts of the Kurdistan Region): night frost and snow make low-temperature flexibility important; SBS-modified membranes and careful edge detailing are recommended.

Thermal insulation and inverted roofs

Building regulations in most Gulf states require roof thermal insulation, so the waterproofing is designed as part of a complete roof system. In a conventional roof, insulation boards go on the slab, then the falls, then the waterproofing on top, which is exposed and needs sun protection. In an inverted roof, the waterproofing, either system, goes on the slab after the falls, and extruded polystyrene boards that do not absorb water are laid over it, followed by a filter layer and gravel or pavers. This protects the waterproofing from sun, impact and temperature swings and greatly extends its life, but the flood test must be carried out before the boards go down. The designer selects the arrangement, and the contractor must follow it without shortcuts.

Cost: price per square metre is not enough

Cost structure of bitumen and jute

  • A larger share of labour and time: melting, layering, harder work.
  • The quantity of bitumen varies and directly affects the price.
  • On exposed roofs, protection is a separate cost.

Cost structure of a membrane

  • A larger share of factory materials: roll price by brand and thickness.
  • Less labour and time.
  • Foil-faced membranes usually need no separate protection on exposed roofs.

Costs common to both, such as stripping old waterproofing, falls, fillets, parapet and drain details and disposal, are often a significant share. For a fair comparison, ask the contractor to price both options for the same roof with the same preparation.

Life-cycle cost

Experienced clients do not look only at today's price; they ask what the waterproofing will cost over its life: installation, annual maintenance (cleaning, coatings, patches), leak repairs and damage inside the building, and replacement at the end of its life. A cheap exposed bitumen roof that needs patching every year may cost more overall than a membrane that runs for years without trouble. Conversely, a cheap, poorly installed membrane under tiles can be the most expensive choice of all, because repairing it means breaking out the tiles. Choose the system that suits how the roof is used, and buy it with proper workmanship.

What should the client check in each system?

Bitumen and jute

  • The bitumen grade on the packaging matches the contract.
  • The jute is new and dry, not second-hand.
  • The bitumen does not give off heavy smoke or a burnt smell.
  • The jute is laid flat and fully saturated.
  • The second layer is at right angles to, or offset from, the first.
  • No fibres are visible in the final coat.
  • Protection is applied immediately.

Membrane

  • Brand, thickness and finish on the roll match the contract.
  • Primer has been applied and is dry.
  • Rolls start at the bottom of the slope.
  • Laps are at least 100 mm.
  • An even bead of bitumen shows along every lap.
  • End laps in adjacent rows are staggered.
  • No burnt foil or torn membrane.

In both systems, upstands, drain and pipe details and the flood test are common and critical. Dated photographs of each stage, especially before layers are covered, are extremely useful for warranty follow-up.

Common myths

"A membrane is always better than bitumen and jute."

No. On exposed roofs a membrane is usually the better choice, but under finishes, in wet areas and on busy surfaces, a well-built bitumen and jute system can perform better. "Better" depends on the location.

"Bitumen and jute is obsolete."

No. It is still used seriously on large projects, in foundations and under finishes. What is obsolete is leaving it exposed to the sun.

"A thicker membrane is a better membrane."

Not necessarily. Bitumen quality, reinforcement and manufacturing consistency matter more than a few tenths of a millimetre.

"More layers mean better waterproofing."

Not always. A new layer on top of rotten old waterproofing only adds weight and problems. Good waterproofing means a sound substrate and correct installation, not a stack of layers.

"With good waterproofing, falls don't matter."

Wrong. Ponding shortens the life of both systems. Correct falls are a precondition for either.

"A long warranty means good work."

A warranty only has value when it is written and states exactly what it covers.

Combining the two systems

  • Bitumen and jute at details, membrane on the field: drains, corners, pipes and plinths are reinforced with bitumen and jute, and the main area is covered with membrane.
  • Bitumen and jute as a base layer, foil-faced membrane as the cap sheet: for sensitive or large roofs.
  • A membrane over sound old bitumen and jute: when the old system is still sound, dry and bonded, it can be cleaned, primed and overlaid.

In every case, the junction between the two systems must be lapped and torched correctly, and the built-up layer must be cool and clean before the membrane is applied.

Five questions for the final decision

  1. Will another finish go on top?If not and the roof stays exposed, a foil-faced membrane comes first. If yes (tiles, screed, asphalt), both are options; go to the next question.
  2. How detailed and irregular is the surface?The more details, the more sense bitumen and jute makes, or at least reinforcing details with it.
  3. Will the waterproofing be accessible later?In foundations, basements and under tiles it will not; continuity and flood testing matter more, and a multi-layer system is preferable.
  4. How tight is the programme?For occupied buildings where time matters, a membrane is usually faster.
  5. Can you supervise closely during the work?If not, a membrane, whose material quality is guaranteed by the factory, carries less risk; if yes, bitumen and jute with a skilled crew is equally reliable.

When neither is the answer

Membranes and bitumen and jute are the most common roof waterproofing systems in the region, but they are not the best choice everywhere. Liquid-applied bituminous or polymer coatings, flexible cementitious coatings and synthetic sheets such as PVC or TPO each have advantages in particular conditions: very busy surfaces where open flames are not allowed, roofs that must stay extremely light, water tanks and swimming pools that need specific certifications, or decks with heavy traffic that need a wearing surface. Where hot works are prohibited, for example on some occupied hospitals, fuel stations or buildings with sensitive equipment, a cold-applied system may be required by the site rules. In those cases the right system should be chosen with the designer, and the same principles still apply: a sound, dry substrate, correct falls, careful details and a test before covering.

Illustrative scenarios

To make the logic concrete, here are some typical situations. They are illustrative examples of how the choice is made, not specific projects:

A four-storey residential building with an exposed roof and several AC units

The roof takes direct sun and has several AC plinths, pipes and a water tank. The logical choice: a foil-faced APP membrane on the field, plinths with fillets and individual flashings, and drains reinforced with bitumen and jute where needed. Old waterproofing that has failed is stripped first.

A villa with a large tiled roof terrace

The waterproofing is hidden under tiles and the terrace has many details: door thresholds, fixed planters, balustrades and floor drains. Two-layer bitumen and jute or a plain membrane, with enough upstand under the door thresholds, a flood test and a separation layer before tiling.

A warehouse roof in a logistics zone

A large, low-slope roof over stock that must stay dry and operations that cannot stop. A two-layer membrane installed in phases so part of the warehouse is always usable, with vents and drains reinforced by bitumen and jute.

A new building with a two-level basement near the coast

The foundation and basement waterproofing will be inaccessible after backfilling, and groundwater may be saline. Multi-layer bitumen and jute or a suitable membrane under the raft and on the walls, with a careful junction at the raft edge and protection before backfilling.

Common rules for a long life, whichever system you choose

  • A dry, clean substrate free of sharp objects.
  • Correct falls to drains or floor drains, with no hollows.
  • Fillets at every internal corner.
  • Primer over the whole surface, fully dried.
  • Upstands of at least 200–300 mm with a sealed top edge.
  • Careful detailing at drains, pipes and plinths.
  • A flood test before the surface is covered.
  • Protection against sun and impact.
  • Regular inspection and maintenance, especially drain cleaning after dust storms.

Older buildings and refurbishment

On older buildings, roofs have often been waterproofed several times: an old bitumen system, a membrane on top, sometimes another membrane over that. Before choosing the new system, decide what happens to the old layers. Stacked layers add weight to the slab, and moisture trapped between them blisters any new waterproofing. In most cases, stripping failed layers, repairing the substrate, correcting the falls and then installing one sound system is more economical than adding yet another layer over existing problems. A refurbishment that converts a roof into a tiled terrace is a good opportunity to install the buried waterproofing with full care and a flood test.

Specifying the system in tender documents

Many disputes about waterproofing start with a vague line in the bill of quantities, such as "waterproofing to roof" or "two-layer bitumen". A good specification, whichever system is chosen, states at least:

  • The system: single or two-layer membrane, or bitumen and jute with the number of layers.
  • The materials: membrane type (APP or SBS), reinforcement, thickness and finish; or bitumen grade and fabric type.
  • Preparation: stripping, substrate repair, falls, fillets and primer.
  • Details: upstand height, termination at the top edge, drain, penetration and expansion joint details, preferably with drawings.
  • Testing: flood test duration and who witnesses it.
  • Protection: type of protective layer and when it is installed.
  • Measurement: whether upstands are measured separately, and how laps and waste are treated.
  • Warranty: what it covers, for how long and under which conditions.

With a specification like this, quotations become comparable and the supervisor has clear criteria for acceptance.

Maintenance plan by system

Bitumen and jute

  • Inspect twice a year and after dust storms.
  • Check the protective layer: renew reflective coatings, repair loose tiles.
  • Look for crazing, blisters and bitumen creeping at upstands.
  • Patch damaged areas promptly with bitumen and jute.

Membrane

  • Inspect twice a year and after dust storms.
  • Check laps and upstand terminations.
  • Watch the foil: where it is worn, apply a compatible reflective coating.
  • Patch punctures promptly with membrane.

For both, keep drains clear, sweep sand and silt from the surface, and make sure other trades do not puncture the waterproofing when installing or moving equipment.

Comparing contractors' proposals

When one proposal offers bitumen and jute and another a membrane, ask both:

  1. Is stripping old waterproofing and disposal included?
  2. Are falls and fillets included?
  3. For bitumen and jute: which bitumen grade, how many layers, which fabric? For a membrane: which brand, reinforcement, finish and thickness?
  4. How high are the upstands, and how is their top edge protected?
  5. How are drains, pipes and plinths detailed?
  6. Is protection (for exposed bitumen and jute) included?
  7. Will a flood test be carried out?
  8. Is the warranty written, and what does it cover?

Have a leak? Find the cause first

Many people asking "membrane or bitumen and jute?" are actually dealing with a leak and assume that changing the type of waterproofing will solve it. But if the cause is poor falls, a blocked drain, an open upstand or mechanical damage, new waterproofing of any type will leak again. Diagnose before you decide.

Frequently asked questions

Which lasts longer, a membrane or bitumen and jute?

Neither has a fixed life. An exposed foil-faced membrane and bitumen and jute under tiles both last a long time when properly installed. Exposed, unprotected bitumen and jute has the shortest life. Workmanship, falls, protection and maintenance matter more than the system.

Which is better for an exposed roof in the Gulf?

For most exposed roofs, a foil-faced APP membrane, because of its speed, uniformity and resistance to sun and heat.

Under tiles, should I use bitumen and jute or a membrane?

Both are suitable. Bitumen and jute offers full bond and continuity; a plain membrane is faster. In both cases, a flood test and a separation layer before tiling are essential.

Which is better for bathrooms?

Bitumen and jute is very common thanks to its flexibility in small, busy spaces; plain membranes are also used with careful detailing. Sealing pipes and floor drains matters more than the system.

Can the two systems be used together?

Yes, for example bitumen and jute at details with membrane on the field, or bitumen and jute as a base layer under a foil-faced cap sheet. The junction must be executed carefully.

Can a membrane be laid over old bitumen and jute?

If the old system is sound, dry and fully bonded, usually yes, after cleaning and priming. A rotten or blistered system must be removed.

Which is cheaper?

It depends on the bitumen grade, membrane brand, preparation and details. Bitumen and jute has a larger labour share, a membrane a larger materials share. Compare both for the same roof with the same preparation.

Do the fumes from hot bitumen affect an occupied building?

Melting bitumen on site produces fumes and odour during the work. The boiler must be kept away from windows and AC intakes, and work scheduled sensibly. Once cooled, the odour fades quickly. Where this is a concern, a membrane gives a cleaner site.

Conclusion

The short answer to "Isogam membrane or bitumen and jute?" is: for exposed roofs, usually a foil-faced membrane; under finishes, in foundations, in wet areas and on busy surfaces, bitumen and jute is an excellent option; and on many projects a combination of both gives the best result. The full answer depends on your building: location, finish, details, climate and budget. And more important than the type of waterproofing is the quality of installation: a dry substrate, correct falls, primer, careful details, a flood test and protection.
