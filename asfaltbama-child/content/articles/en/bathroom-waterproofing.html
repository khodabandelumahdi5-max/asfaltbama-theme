Bathroom and wet area waterproofing is one of those jobs you never think about when it is done right, and that causes years of trouble when it is done wrong. Water used every day in bathrooms, showers and kitchens, once it gets past the waterproofing, slowly soaks the slab of the unit below, ruins its plaster and paint, brings mould and, in apartment buildings, hotels and compounds, usually ends in disputes. Repairing it means breaking out tiles and screed: weeks of disruption and a cost many times higher than getting the waterproofing right in the first place.

This guide covers bathroom and wet area waterproofing from choosing the system and coordinating with plumbing to the step-by-step installation, the seven points where most leaks occur, flood testing, the signs of a leak, diagnosis and repair. It is written for developers, contractors, fit-out managers, hotel and facility operators and owners in Iraq and the Gulf who want bathrooms that stay watertight for years.

Key points at a glance

  • The floor must be laid to falls towards the floor drain before waterproofing.
  • The waterproofing must turn up the walls, and much higher within the shower zone.
  • The floor drain and every pipe need dedicated sealing details; most leaks start there.
  • A 24–48 hour flood test before tiling is not optional.
  • Pipework must be pressure-tested before waterproofing; a pipe leak beneath the membrane bypasses it entirely.

Why bathrooms leak

  • Daily, repeated water: unlike a roof, which is wet only when it rains, a bathroom floor gets wet every day.
  • Many penetrations: hot and cold supply, soil and waste pipes, floor drains, WC and basin connections all pass through the floor and walls.
  • Many corners and details: small spaces with corners, door thresholds, shower trays or level-access showers and sometimes baths.
  • Hidden waterproofing: it is buried under screed and tiles, so defects show only when the ceiling below gets wet.
  • Several trades: plumbers, waterproofers, tilers and fixture installers can each unintentionally damage the others' work.

Floor build-up in a wet area

  1. Structural slabConcrete or rib-and-block slab, often set down in wet areas to accommodate pipes and falls.
  2. Pipework and infillDrainage pipes on the slab, with the space between them filled.
  3. Screed to fallsA smooth surface falling to the floor drain.
  4. WaterproofingOn the floor and turned up the walls.
  5. Protection or bedding screedOver the waterproofing.
  6. Tile adhesive and tilesFinished floor, also falling to the drain.

In some details a second waterproofing layer is placed on the slab beneath the infill, so that if a pipe in the infill leaks, the water does not reach the slab. This two-layer detail is recommended over sensitive spaces.

In practice, a common and reliable combination is bitumen and jute or a bitumen membrane on the floor and lower walls, and a liquid or flexible cementitious membrane on the shower walls up to a greater height, with a compatible lap between the two. In hotels and large residential projects in the Gulf, fully cementitious or liquid systems for floors and walls are also common, provided the specified thickness and reinforcement at corners are respected.

Before starting: coordinate with plumbing and design

  • Pressure-test the supply pipes before they are covered; a leak beneath the waterproofing bypasses it and wets the slab below directly.
  • Test the drainage pipes and joints.
  • Fix the positions of drains and WCs; moving them after waterproofing means cutting it.
  • Agree the finished floor level so the build-up matches the door threshold and water cannot spill into the corridor.
  • Define the shower zone: tray, level-access or linear drain, and the required wall waterproofing height.
  • Sequence the trades and make clear who is responsible for later damage.

Choosing the floor drain

  • Drains with a waterproofing flange or clamping ring give a far more reliable connection than simple drains where the membrane is only stuck to a round pipe.
  • Dual-level drains also collect water that has passed through tile joints and runs on the membrane; valuable in heavily used showers.
  • Height-adjustable gratings allow precise levelling with the tiles without disturbing the membrane connection.
  • Linear drains, common in level-access showers, need the manufacturer's membrane connection details and parts.
  • Traps: in rarely used bathrooms and hotel rooms that stand empty, traps dry out and cause odours; drains with suitable trap designs help.

The drain must be fixed in its final position before the screed, and the falls formed to it.

Installing a liquid under-tile membrane

  1. Suitable substrateSound cement render or screed, or boards designed for wet areas; gypsum plaster is not suitable under tiles in wet zones.
  2. PrimerAs specified by the manufacturer, to even out absorbency.
  3. Reinforcing tape at cornersTape or fabric embedded in the first coat at wall-floor junctions, corners and around pipes.
  4. Pipe collarsPreformed collars around mixer and shower pipes.
  5. At least two coatsWith drying time between coats, preferably at right angles, to reach the required dry thickness.
  6. Thickness controlConsumption per m² as specified; thin films crack early, and in hot, dry conditions the material may need protection from drying too fast.
  7. Lap with the floor waterproofingIf the floor has a bitumen system, the wall membrane must lap compatibly over its upstand.
  8. Compatible tile adhesiveTile only after full cure, with an adhesive compatible with the membrane.

Step-by-step installation

1. Preparation and strip-out (in refurbishments)

Tiles, screed and old waterproofing are removed from the floor and the necessary part of the walls down to sound slab and substrate. Check the slab under an old bathroom; long-term leaks may have corroded reinforcement or softened screed.

2. Plumbing and testing

New pipework is installed and pressure-tested and fixed firmly so it does not move during the following stages.

3. Infill and levelling

The space between pipes is filled and the surface prepared for the screed. Lightweight fills hold water if they get wet, which is why the waterproofing above them must be perfect.

4. Screed to falls

A screed with a fall of about 1–2 percent to the drain, smooth and without hollows. A hollow under the membrane is where water passing through tile joints collects. The screed must be dry before waterproofing.

5. Fillets

Wall-floor junctions, shower kerbs and plinths are filleted so the waterproofing does not crack in sharp angles.

6. Primer

The appropriate primer for the system is applied to dry surfaces.

7. Floor waterproofing

Bitumen and jute, membrane or liquid system on the floor. For membranes, adequate, fully fused laps; for bitumen and jute, fully saturated fabric without wrinkles; for liquids, the specified thickness and coats with reinforcement at corners.

8. Upstands

The waterproofing turns up all walls at least 200–300 mm above finished floor level. Within the shower zone, wall waterproofing continues much higher, typically to around shower-head height or more. Beside baths, walls are waterproofed above the bath rim.

9. Floor drain

The most important point. The waterproofing must connect fully and continuously to the drain body so water running on the membrane enters the drain and has no path beneath it.

10. Pipes

Every pipe through the floor or wall, including WC, basin, shower and bath connections, receives a collar of waterproofing turned up and secured on the pipe.

11. Door threshold

The floor waterproofing continues under the door threshold and turns up beside the frame. The finished bathroom floor should sit slightly below the corridor, or the threshold should be high enough to stop water spilling out.

12. Flood test

The drain is plugged and the floor flooded to a shallow depth, below the upstand height, for 24–48 hours while the ceiling below is inspected. The result is recorded.

13. Protection and tiling

After a successful test, a protection or bedding screed goes over the waterproofing and tiling follows, falling to the drain. Tilers must not puncture the waterproofing with nails, screws or tools; any fixing in the waterproofed zone must be sealed.

Wet areas over sensitive spaces

Where a bathroom sits above a shop, office, electrical room, storage or another unit's kitchen, or above hotel public areas, a two-layer system is recommended: one layer on the slab beneath the infill and pipes, and a second on the screed beneath the tiles. The lower layer keeps water from any pipe leak in the infill away from the slab; sometimes a drainage path from the lower layer to the drain is also provided.

On projects with many bathrooms, such as hotels and residential towers, completing and testing a mock-up bathroom before the rest reveals mistakes early and sets the standard for every trade.

The seven points where most leaks occur

  1. Waterproofing-to-drain connection: the most common leak point.
  2. Around WC and soil pipes: especially when the WC position was moved after waterproofing.
  3. Shower corners: where water hits the walls directly and constantly.
  4. Door thresholds: water passes under the threshold to the corridor or under the membrane to the slab.
  5. Wall-mounted pipes and mixers: concealed valve bodies inside the wall.
  6. Around and under baths: water gets behind the bath through the joint with the wall.
  7. Accessory fixings: towel rails, shelves and mirrors fixed through the shower wall after waterproofing.

Level-access showers, baths and linear drains

Modern bathrooms, particularly in hotels and premium residences in the Gulf, often have level-access showers and linear drains. They are attractive and practical but more demanding: falls must be more precise, the whole bathroom floor is exposed to spray, and the membrane connection to the linear drain needs specific details. Waterproof the entire floor, the shower walls to full height and control falls carefully. With baths, the walls behind and beside the bath are waterproofed above the rim and the bath-to-tile joint is sealed with a flexible sanitary sealant.

Kitchens, laundries and terraces

  • Kitchens: under sinks and dishwashers, and in kitchens with floor drains, floor waterproofing is recommended; a leaking hose or trap can silently wet the ceiling below.
  • Laundries: a floor drain and floor waterproofing under the washing machine prevent damage from hose failures.
  • Terraces and balconies: exposed to rain and washing, with the door threshold as the critical point; treat them as small roofs.

Why the flood test is not optional

  • Keep the water below the upstand height.
  • Plug the drain securely.
  • Hold for 24–48 hours and inspect the ceiling below, especially under drains and pipes.
  • Mark the water level at the start and end; a noticeable drop beyond evaporation is a warning. In the Gulf heat, evaporation can be significant, so compare with a reference container of water.
  • Drain and let the surface dry before tiling.

Signs of a bathroom leak

  • Stains, damp or blistered plaster on the ceiling below, usually under the drain, WC or shower.
  • Darkened or crumbling floor grout, especially along walls and around the drain.
  • Loose tiles or tiles that sound hollow.
  • Musty smell or mould in the bathroom or on the wall behind it.
  • Damp at the base of the wall outside the bathroom, in the corridor or adjoining room.
  • Mould in shower corners that returns after cleaning.

Diagnosis: pipe leak or waterproofing failure?

  1. Meter testClose all taps and watch the water meter. If it moves without use, the leak is in pressurised pipework, not the waterproofing.
  2. Usage testStop using the shower for a few days and use only the basin, then the reverse; changes in the stain show which part is at fault.
  3. Floor testPlug the drain, pour a little water on the floor and hold it for a few hours. If the stain below becomes active, the floor waterproofing or drain connection has failed.
  4. Drain testPour water directly into the drain without wetting the floor. If a leak appears, the problem is the drain pipe or its connection.
  5. Grout inspectionCracked, crumbling grout lets water under the tiles and keeps the waterproofing under constant load.

Repairs: possible without removing tiles?

Sometimes, within limits. If the waterproofing is intact and the problem is only failed grout or sanitary sealant, regrouting (for example with epoxy grout) and replacing silicone joints can solve it, and penetrating sealers may help for a while. But if the waterproofing is damaged, the drain was not connected properly or there is no waterproofing at all, no surface treatment replaces it; tiles must come up and the area be re-waterproofed.

Wall substrates in wet areas

  • Gypsum plaster is not suitable under tiles in wet zones; it softens with moisture and loses adhesion.
  • Cement render is a good substrate for liquid or cementitious membranes and tiles if sound and fully cured.
  • Lightweight partitions must use boards designed for wet areas, such as cement boards, with joints taped and sealed.
  • Lightweight blocks absorb water; their surface must be rendered before waterproofing.

Ventilation, mould and condensation

Many dark stains on bathroom ceilings are not leaks but condensation. Steam condenses on cold surfaces; in air-conditioned buildings in the Gulf, warm humid air meeting cold, air-conditioned surfaces has the same effect. Condensation mould typically appears on the bathroom's own ceiling and upper walls and in cold corners, while leak stains appear below the bathroom and at the base of walls. An extract fan that runs for a while after showering and adequate insulation reduce condensation; waterproofing cannot cure it.

Dividing responsibilities in the contract

  • Plumber: pipework and drains, pressure tests and sealed joints before waterproofing.
  • Waterproofer: fillets, primer, floor and wall waterproofing, drain and pipe details, flood test and record.
  • Tiler: bedding and tiles to falls, drain levelling, keeping the waterproofing intact and filling joints.
  • Fixture installer: fixing without puncturing the waterproofing or with sealed fixings, sanitary sealant.
  • Client or supervisor: accepting each stage before the next and keeping records.

What affects the cost?

  • Area and number of bathrooms: doing all wet areas together spreads fixed costs.
  • System and materials.
  • Details: number of pipes, drain type, level-access showers, baths.
  • Wall waterproofing height, especially in showers.
  • Preparation: strip-out, disposal and slab repairs in refurbishments.
  • Flood testing and protection.

The cost per square metre is higher than on roofs because of the time per metre, but trivial compared with breaking out tiles and repairing the ceiling below.

Hotels, serviced apartments and repeat bathrooms

A hotel or apartment block may contain dozens or hundreds of nearly identical bathrooms. That repetition is an opportunity and a risk at the same time. If the detail is right, the same correct method is repeated everywhere; if one detail is wrong, the same defect is repeated in every room and usually appears only after handover, when guests or tenants are already living in the building.

For repeat bathrooms we recommend the following approach:

  • A typical-bathroom drawing: one sheet showing falls, drain position, upstand heights, the shower wall height, the threshold detail and every pipe penetration, agreed before work starts.
  • A standard kit of accessories: the same drain type, the same pipe collars and the same corner pieces in every room, so the crews do not improvise with whatever is on site.
  • A dedicated crew per floor: the same trained team covers a zone, so quality is traceable and training is not lost with every new worker.
  • A room-by-room record: for each bathroom a simple sheet records the date of waterproofing, the flood test result and who signed it off. When a leak appears years later, this record saves days of investigation.

In hotels the programme pressure is usually high and the temptation to tile before the flood test is strong. It is exactly in these projects that skipping the test is most expensive, because a defect repeated in fifty rooms means fifty ceilings to open.

Mock-up and inspection hold points

On any project with more than a handful of bathrooms, the first room should be completed as a mock-up. The mock-up is waterproofed, flood-tested and inspected by the consultant, the main contractor and the waterproofing contractor together. Details that look fine on paper often need adjusting once the real drain, the real pipes and the real wall thickness are in place: the upstand height at the door, the transition at the shower niche or the connection to a wall-hung WC frame.

After the mock-up is approved, it becomes the reference for all other rooms. We then suggest a small number of inspection hold points, where work cannot continue until the stage is checked:

  1. Substrate and falls: screed cured, falls checked with a level or a bucket of water, pipes pressure-tested and fixed.
  2. Details before the main layer: fillets, drain and pipe collars, corner reinforcement.
  3. Completed membrane: upstands, shower walls and threshold complete, no damage.
  4. Flood test: water held for the agreed period, level marked, ceiling below checked.
  5. Protection and handover to tiling: protective screed or tile adhesive applied without puncturing the membrane.

Each hold point takes only minutes when the work is right, and it gives everyone a clear moment to stop a defect before it is covered.

Cementitious and liquid membranes in hot climates

In the Gulf, many fit-out specifications call for flexible cementitious or liquid-applied membranes in bathrooms rather than bitumen sheets. These systems work well when their specific requirements are respected, and heat makes some of those requirements more critical:

  • Working time: in hot, dry air, cementitious slurries and some liquid membranes skin over quickly. Mix smaller batches, keep material out of direct sun and avoid working on a hot substrate.
  • Substrate moisture: a dusty, very dry substrate draws water out of a cementitious coat and weakens adhesion. Pre-dampening (without standing water) is often required by the manufacturer.
  • Thickness: the most common failure is simply a membrane that is too thin. Measure consumption per square metre and check wet-film thickness; two or more coats are usually needed.
  • Reinforcement: fleece or mesh at corners, junctions and around drains is not optional; it bridges the movement that cracks the coating.
  • Curing before testing: the flood test can only be done after the curing time given in the technical data sheet, which may change with temperature.

Tip

Ask the supplier for the technical data sheet and follow its figures for consumption, coats and curing. Where a site condition falls outside those figures, such as very high temperature, discuss it before work starts rather than after a failed test.

Handover documents

At the end of the job, the owner or main contractor should receive a short file for the wet areas: the materials used and their data sheets, photos of each stage in a sample of rooms (or all rooms on smaller projects), flood test records with dates and the scope and conditions of any warranty in writing. These documents cost little to prepare and are extremely useful when a question arises years later, when a tenant changes or when the building is sold.

Common mistakes

  1. Waterproofing before the pipework is pressure-tested.
  2. Waterproofing on a wet screed or one with hollows.
  3. Short upstands, especially in showers.
  4. A weak connection to the floor drain.
  5. Cutting the waterproofing to move pipes or fix accessories without sealing.
  6. Skipping the flood test to start tiling sooner.
  7. Tiling with falls towards the door.
  8. Unsealed or poorly sealed bath-to-tile joints.
  9. Finished bathroom floor level with or above the corridor.
  10. No coordination between plumbers, waterproofers and tilers.

Maintenance

  • Keep grout sound and replace cracked or crumbling grout promptly.
  • Replace silicone joints at baths, showers and basins when they darken or detach.
  • Keep drains clean so water does not stand on the floor.
  • Avoid excessive use of harsh cleaners that attack grout.
  • Keep extract ventilation working.
  • Repair any leaking pipe, tap or trap immediately.
  • Use sealed fixings when adding accessories to shower walls.

Frequently asked questions

Can a bathroom be waterproofed without removing tiles?

If the problem is only grout or sanitary sealant, replacing them may be enough. If the waterproofing beneath the tiles is damaged or missing, no surface coating replaces it and the tiles must come up.

How long does bathroom waterproofing take?

The waterproofing itself usually takes about a day, but screed and primer drying before, and a 24–48 hour flood test after, must be allowed for.

How high should the waterproofing go on the walls?

At least 200–300 mm above finished floor level on general walls; much higher in the shower zone, typically to around shower-head height or more; and above the rim beside baths.

Should the whole floor be waterproofed or only the shower?

The whole floor. Water from the shower, cleaning and fixture leaks spreads across the floor and the drain collects it at one point.

Which system is best?

Bitumen systems on the floor and lower walls are common and reliable; liquid or flexible cementitious membranes suit shower walls. A combination with a proper lap usually gives the best result. Many Gulf projects use fully cementitious or liquid systems successfully when thickness and details are right.

Do level-access showers need special waterproofing?

Yes: the whole floor is exposed to water and falls must be precise, so full-floor waterproofing, a suitable (usually linear) drain with proper connection details and full-height shower wall waterproofing are essential.

The ceiling below is stained; how do we know it is our bathroom?

Use the meter, usage, floor and drain tests described above. Where and when the stain appears are also important clues.

Do kitchens need floor waterproofing?

In kitchens with floor drains, and under sinks and dishwashers, it is recommended; hose and trap leaks can silently damage the ceiling below.

How long should the flood test last?

A common practice is at least 24 hours, and on sensitive floors (above offices, shops or electrical rooms) often 48 hours. The water level is marked at the start and the ceiling below is checked during and after the test. Follow the specification or the consultant's requirement where one exists.

Can we tile directly on the membrane?

It depends on the system. Many liquid and cementitious membranes are designed to receive tile adhesive directly; bitumen sheets usually need a protective screed or a compatible bonding layer. The manufacturer's data sheet decides, not habit on site.

Does a wall-hung WC need special attention?

Yes. The frame is fixed through the floor and wall, and its pipe connections pass through the waterproofing zone. Collars around the waste pipe and a continuous upstand behind the frame are needed before the frame is boxed in.

Is silicone at the tile edges part of the waterproofing?

No. Silicone at the shower tray, bath edge and corners is a maintenance joint that limits how much water reaches the membrane, but it ages and must be renewed. The real barrier is the membrane beneath the tiles.

Conclusion

Bathroom waterproofing is a small job with big consequences. Pressure-testing pipes first, correct falls, fillets, continuous floor waterproofing with adequate upstands and extra height in showers, a careful connection to drains and pipes, a proper threshold and a flood test before tiling make the difference between a bathroom that works for years and one that ruins the ceiling below.
