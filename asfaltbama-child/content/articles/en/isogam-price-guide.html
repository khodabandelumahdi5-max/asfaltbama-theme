Isogam membrane prices can vary widely from one roll to another, even when two products look almost identical. The reason is that a modified bitumen membrane's quality depends on things you cannot see: the type and quantity of bitumen, the polymer modifier, the type and weight of the reinforcement, the real thickness and the quality of the surface finish. On top of that, what a client finally pays is not only the roll price; the installed price also depends on roof preparation, details, access and the quality of the workmanship.

This guide explains what determines the price of Isogam and other APP/SBS membranes, the types available and what each is for, simple checks to recognise a good membrane, the most common tricks in the market, exactly how many rolls your roof needs, and how to compare contractors' quotations fairly. It is written for owners, procurement teams and main contractors in Iraq and the Gulf, where membranes are imported from several countries and quality varies more than labels suggest. Because prices change with the bitumen market and shipping costs, we do not quote figures; instead, we give you the tools to make the right decision.

Key points at a glance

Two prices: the roll and the installed square metre

Roll price (materials)

The price a supplier or distributor charges per roll. It depends on type, thickness, brand, bitumen prices and, in the Gulf and Iraq, on freight, customs and the supply chain. Anyone buying materials directly or checking a contractor's proposal needs this figure.

Installed price (per m²)

The price a contractor quotes for complete waterproofing, usually per square metre. It includes materials (or not), labour, primer, preparation, details, lifting and sometimes testing and warranty. For clients this is the figure that matters, but it is harder to compare.

Many misunderstandings start here: a client asks the market price of a roll, divides it by ten, compares it with the contractor's installed rate and assumes the difference is profit. In reality the installed rate includes laps and waste, primer, gas, labour, preparation, details, lifting and supervision, none of which is in the roll price.

Eight factors that set the price of a roll

1. Thickness and weight

Common membranes have a nominal thickness of 3 or 4 mm. More thickness means more bitumen, and bitumen is the most expensive component. But the thickness on the label is not always the real thickness. Roll weight is a better indicator: two rolls of the same size and nominal thickness with different weights contain different amounts of bitumen. Reputable manufacturers state the weight per square metre in their data sheets, and a client can check it with simple scales.

2. Reinforcement type and weight

  • Polyester: high tensile and tear strength and good elongation; the safer choice for exposed roofs, trafficked roofs and large structures, and usually more expensive.
  • Glass fibre: good dimensional stability, but more brittle and lower tear resistance.
  • Cheaper carriers: used in low-cost products; weaker against rot and tension.

The weight of the reinforcement (g/m²) also matters; lighter carriers are cheaper and weaker.

3. Bitumen type and quality

The bitumen is modified with polymers, APP or SBS, so it does not flow in heat or crack in cold. The type and dosage of polymer affect performance across temperatures and the price. A common trick in cheap membranes is adding mineral fillers to the bitumen, which maintains weight and thickness but reduces flexibility and durability. Such a membrane looks fine on delivery and cracks after a few seasons of Gulf sun.

4. Surface finish

Foil-faced membranes cost more than plain ones because of the foil and the more complex production. Foil quality varies too: thin or poorly bonded foil cracks and peels quickly under strong sun. Mineral granules, sand and polyethylene film have different costs.

5. Brand, certification and standards

Established manufacturers usually have tighter quality control, written technical data and more consistent rolls, and their prices are slightly higher. Product certification and test results against recognised standards, such as EN 13707 for reinforced bitumen roofing sheets or the ASTM D6222 family for APP membranes, are signs of a product you can rely on. Remember that "Isogam" began as a brand name and is now used for all bituminous roll membranes, so the name alone says nothing about quality.

6. Roll dimensions

The most common roll is 1 m wide and 10 m long, but shorter rolls exist. Always compare prices per square metre, not per roll.

7. Bitumen market price

Bitumen is the main raw material, and its price follows oil and refinery markets. Membrane prices are therefore not fixed and can change within weeks; contractors' quotations usually have a validity period.

8. Freight, quantity and season

Transport from factory to site, import duties where applicable, quantity (container or small lots) and the working season all affect the final roll price. For projects in Iraq and the Gulf, freight and lead times can be a significant part of the cost.

Foil-faced or plain?

If the roof stays exposed, a foil-faced membrane is usually right. The aluminium foil reflects sunlight, keeps the surface cooler and protects the bitumen from UV, which means less cracking and a longer life, particularly important where roofs reach extreme temperatures in summer. A plain membrane on an exposed roof must be coated with an aluminium paint designed for bitumen, renewed every few years.

If tiles, mortar or a screed go on top, a plain membrane is sufficient; foil has no function under mortar and can even reduce the bond of the layer above. Spend the saving on better reinforcement or bitumen instead.

Technical data to look for

  • Heat resistance (flow resistance): the temperature at which the bitumen does not flow on a slope. The higher, the better for hot roofs.
  • Cold flexibility: the lowest temperature at which the membrane bends without cracking. The lower, the better for cold regions and for long-term ageing.
  • Tensile strength and elongation: how much force and stretch the membrane takes before breaking; strongly linked to reinforcement.
  • Tear and puncture resistance: important for roofs with traffic and equipment.
  • Watertightness: the membrane must pass a water pressure test.
  • Mass per square metre and thickness: to verify bitumen content.

If a supplier cannot provide a data sheet, or its figures do not match the roll label, treat the product with caution. On consultant-supervised projects, the data sheet and certificates are part of the material submittal.

Material submittals on consultant-supervised projects

On projects in the Gulf and on larger projects in Iraq, the membrane is normally approved by the consultant before it reaches site. A typical material submittal includes the manufacturer's technical data sheet, test certificates against the specified standard, product approvals required by the local authority where applicable, the manufacturer's installation instructions, samples, and sometimes a letter confirming the installer's experience with the product. Preparing a complete submittal takes time, but it protects everyone: the client knows exactly what will be installed, and the contractor has an approved reference if questions arise later. A quotation for a supervised project should therefore allow for submittals, inspections and test records, and the approved product must not be substituted without a new approval.

Price and performance in extreme heat

Gulf and southern Iraqi summers put membranes under more thermal stress than most climates. A dark, unprotected membrane can reach extreme surface temperatures, the bitumen softens, foot traffic leaves marks and heavy equipment legs sink in. Several choices that add a little to the price pay back quickly in this climate: an APP formulation with a high flow-resistance temperature, a good-quality foil that stays bonded, polyester reinforcement that tolerates daily movement, and plinths or protected bases for equipment. Where the roof is insulated as an inverted roof, the membrane is shielded from heat and a plain grade under the insulation can be the more economical choice.

Twelve simple checks for membrane quality

  1. Label and dataEach roll or pack should show the manufacturer, type, thickness, reinforcement, dimensions and batch or production date. Unlabelled rolls are a risk.
  2. Roll weightWeigh a few rolls and compare with the data sheet. Large differences from the data sheet or between rolls indicate less bitumen or inconsistent production.
  3. Uniform thicknessCheck the roll edge at several points; thickness should be even across width and length.
  4. Cross-sectionCut a small piece: the reinforcement should sit in the middle of the thickness, with even bitumen on both sides.
  5. Cold bendIn cool conditions, bend a narrow strip slowly around a pipe or finger. A good membrane bends without cracking; one bulked with fillers shows fine cracks.
  6. Bitumen appearance and odourThe bitumen should be uniform, without voids or coarse particles. A very strong solvent or oil smell is unusual.
  7. Foil bondThe foil should be fully bonded and not peel easily with a fingernail, and free of wrinkles and tears.
  8. Behaviour under the torchA good membrane melts evenly to a glossy, fluid, sticky surface; a poor one may smoke heavily, burn or melt reluctantly.
  9. Storage conditionsRolls stored for long periods in the sun, lying down or stacked may be deformed or stuck together.
  10. Production dateVery old stock kept in poor conditions may have lost properties.
  11. InvoiceBuy with an invoice that states the specification; it matters for warranty and traceability.
  12. ConsistencyAll rolls on one project should be the same type and ideally the same batch.

Common tricks in the membrane market

  • Thinner than stated: a roll labelled 4 mm that is actually thinner. Weighing and measuring the edge helps.
  • Filler-bulked bitumen: weight is maintained but flexibility and durability drop. The cold bend test is a good indicator.
  • Fake or swapped labels: a well-known brand's label on an unknown product. Buy from authorised distributors and check packaging consistency.
  • Different reinforcement from the label: a cheaper carrier instead of polyester. In a cross-section, polyester looks felt-like and stretchy.
  • Silent substitution on site: a contractor prices a specified brand but installs another. Being present when materials arrive and photographing labels reduces this risk; on larger projects, material inspection requests with the consultant do the same.

How many rolls does my roof need?

Rolls = (roof plan area + upstand area + detail area) × (1 + waste %) ÷ area per roll

  • Plan area: length × width; split irregular roofs into rectangles.
  • Upstands: perimeter of parapets and stair enclosures × upstand height (usually 0.2–0.3 m).
  • Details: around kerbs, plinths and ducts, the enclosure roof and reinforcing patches at drains.
  • Waste: usually 10–15 percent for laps and cutting; more on busy roofs.
  • Area per roll: 10 m² for a 1 × 10 m roll.

Example 1: a simple roof

  • Plan area: 10 × 8 = 80 m²
  • Perimeter: 36 m; upstands at 0.3 m: 10.8 m²
  • Total 90.8 m²; with 12 percent waste about 101.7 m²
  • Rolls: about 11

Example 2: a roof with a stair enclosure and AC units

  • Roof 12 × 15 m minus enclosure footprint 3 × 4 m: 168 m²
  • Parapet upstands: 54 m × 0.3 = 16.2 m²
  • Enclosure wall upstands: 14 m × 0.3 = 4.2 m²
  • Enclosure roof: 12 m²
  • Plinths and drain patches: about 6 m²
  • Total about 206 m²; with 15 percent waste about 237 m²
  • Rolls: about 24

Example 3: two-layer system

The same roof as a two-layer system needs roughly twice the membrane, about 48 rolls, usually a plain base sheet and a foil-faced cap sheet.

What a site survey measures

An accurate estimate starts with an accurate survey: roof dimensions and irregular sections, the length and height of all parapets and enclosure walls, the enclosure roof, the number and position of drains, pipes, vents and ducts, plinth sizes, the condition and number of old waterproofing layers, and hollows that need correcting. Photographs of details make the quotation more precise. An estimate given without a visit, based only on "approximate roof area", usually ends with variations during the work or with parts of the work quietly left out.

What drives the installed price?

  • Preparation: stripping old membranes or bitumen, disposal and repairs, sometimes taking longer than installing the new membrane.
  • Falls and fillets: where the roof has hollows or corners without fillets.
  • Primer: consumption depends on the substrate.
  • Details: drains, pipes, plinths and parapet lengths; a small, busy roof may cost more per m² than a large, simple one.
  • Number of layers: single or two-layer.
  • Access: building height, lifting rolls, working-hour and site restrictions.
  • Area: on small roofs, mobilisation is a larger share.
  • Protection and services: reflective coating, flood test, written warranty.
  • Location and season: distance to site, summer working hours and peak season demand.
  • Documentation: on supervised projects, submittals, method statements, inspection requests and test records take time and are part of the price.

Why small roofs cost more per square metre

Part of every job's cost is independent of area: mobilising the crew, transporting materials and tools, lifting rolls, setting up and clearing the site. On small roofs this fixed cost is spread over fewer square metres. Small roofs also tend to have more wall length and detail relative to their area, so a larger share of the work is slow, careful detailing. Grouping several small roofs or combining the roof with wet areas or terraces spreads the fixed costs.

Costs by application

Bathrooms and wet areas

Small areas with many details: floor drains, pipes, corners and upstands, especially in shower zones. The cost per square metre is usually higher than on roofs because the time per metre is higher. A plain membrane under screed and tiles is sufficient.

Terraces and balconies

Similar: small areas and many details, with the main cost in upstands and door thresholds.

Foundations and basements

Large areas and relatively simple work on flat blinding, but high sensitivity because the membrane will never be accessible again. The protective layer and the raft-to-wall junction must be priced as well.

Industrial roofs and warehouses

Large areas spread fixed costs, so the rate per m² is often lower than on small roofs. Two-layer systems, phased work to avoid stopping operations and details at vents and rooflights carry their own costs. These projects are often priced together with asphalt paving of yards and roads.

Should the client or the contractor buy the materials?

Client supplies

  • Direct control over brand and quality.
  • Full transparency on material cost.
  • But shortages, excess waste, transport and quality are the client's responsibility.
  • If the roof leaks, disputes between "bad material" and "bad workmanship" are likely.

Contractor supplies

  • Single responsibility for materials and installation; clearer warranty.
  • The contractor manages quantities and waste.
  • The client specifies materials in the contract and checks them on delivery.
  • Usually simpler for the client.

On most projects, contractor supply with exact specifications in the contract and label checks on delivery is the simplest and most transparent route.

Buying for large projects

On developments, compounds, industrial facilities and government projects needing thousands of square metres: buy in one lot or planned deliveries from the same batch so all roofs are consistent; coordinate deliveries with the programme so rolls do not sit for months in the sun; sample and check rolls in each delivery; and, where possible, complete and test a sample roof before the rest. On such projects, a small difference in roll price is trivial compared with the cost of reworking several roofs.

Budgeting a roof waterproofing project

A realistic budget includes more than membrane and labour. When planning, allow for these lines so the project does not stall halfway:

  • Survey and design: a site survey, and on larger projects a specification and detail drawings.
  • Stripping and disposal: removal of old layers and legal disposal of waste.
  • Substrate works: repairs, screed to falls, fillets, kerbs and plinths for equipment.
  • Drainage: new or larger drains, overflows, leaf guards and downpipe repairs where needed.
  • Waterproofing: primer, membrane and reinforcing pieces, with laps and waste.
  • Insulation and protection: insulation boards, filter layer, gravel or pavers, or reflective coating.
  • Temporary works: moving and reinstating AC units, tanks and solar panels; protection of the building below.
  • Testing and documentation: flood test, records and warranty.
  • Contingency: hidden defects often appear once old layers are removed; a contingency avoids difficult decisions mid-job.

Questions to ask a membrane supplier

  1. Can you provide the technical data sheet and test certificates for this exact product?
  2. What is the reinforcement type and weight, and the mass per square metre?
  3. Is it APP or SBS, and what are its heat resistance and cold flexibility values?
  4. What is the roll size and the batch or production date of the stock?
  5. How have the rolls been stored, and can they be delivered in stages to match the programme?
  6. Can all rolls for the project come from the same batch?
  7. Will the invoice state the full specification?

A supplier who answers these questions readily and in writing is usually one you can rely on.

Storing rolls on site

  • Store rolls upright, not lying or stacked.
  • Keep them in the shade; in Gulf heat, the layers of a roll left in the sun stick together.
  • On a flat, dry surface, away from moisture.
  • In cold weather, keep rolls somewhere warmer before unrolling so they do not crack.
  • Keep packaging and labels until use.

Common buying mistakes

  1. Comparing roll prices without checking roll size.
  2. Foil-faced membrane under tiles, or plain membrane exposed without protection.
  3. Relying on the name "Isogam" or appearance without checking data and brand.
  4. Buying without an invoice or recorded specification.
  5. Calculating rolls from roof area alone, without upstands, details and waste, and running short mid-job.
  6. Mixing rolls from different sources on one roof.
  7. Storing rolls in the sun or lying down.
  8. Spending on materials and saving on installation, when workmanship matters as much as materials.

The hidden cost of buying cheap

  • Repeated leak repairs and inspection visits.
  • Damage to slabs, plaster, paint, contents and services.
  • Early replacement of the whole membrane, plus the cost of stripping it.
  • Disputes and management time.

Experienced clients look for the best value, not the lowest price: specified, quality materials, proper installation, a flood test and a written warranty.

Coatings and maintenance costs

The cost of waterproofing is not only the cost on the day of installation. For a fair comparison, include maintenance: a plain membrane on an exposed roof needs a reflective coating renewed every few years, while a foil-faced membrane avoids that cost as long as its foil remains sound. Cleaning drains after dust storms and yearly inspections cost little but noticeably extend life, and repairing a lifting lap early is far cheaper than repairing leak damage. Sometimes the membrane that costs slightly more on day one costs less over its life.

A new membrane over an old one: saving or extra cost?

Laying a new membrane over an old one avoids stripping costs and looks cheaper. If the old membrane is sound, dry and fully bonded, this can work after cleaning, repairs and priming. But if the old layer is rotten, blistered or wet, the new membrane will not last, and today's saving becomes tomorrow's rework, now with two layers to strip. The decision should be made on site after testing the old layer's bond and moisture.

Life-cycle cost: a simple way to compare

To compare two options fairly, estimate for each one: the installed cost today; the expected maintenance over the years you plan to own the building (coatings, inspections, small repairs); the likely cost of leak damage if the system underperforms; and the cost of stripping and replacing it at the end of its life. Divide the total by the number of years you expect it to perform well. This rough exercise often shows that a membrane with better reinforcement, a good foil and proper detailing costs less per year than a cheaper product, particularly on exposed roofs in extreme heat where cheap membranes age fastest. It also shows why preparation and details, which cost little compared with the damage a leak causes, are the last place to save money.

When is a two-layer system worth it?

A two-layer system roughly doubles materials and adds labour. It makes sense on large, low-slope roofs where minor ponding is likely, on industrial roofs and warehouses where a leak causes heavy losses, where the waterproofing will be buried and hard to reach, and on sensitive buildings. For most small residential roofs with correct falls, one layer of a quality membrane properly installed is sufficient, and the money for a second layer is better spent on preparation and details.

Warranty and price

A contractor offering a genuine written warranty has to allow for quality materials, time for details, a flood test and return visits for any repairs. A quote that is both "the cheapest" and promises "the longest warranty" deserves extra scrutiny: what exactly is covered, what voids it, and how reachable is the contractor? A clear written warranty is part of the value of a quotation, not a marketing line.

When to buy and install

In Iraq and the Gulf, the cooler, dry months are the best time to install. If you want the roof ready before the rainy season, start surveys and quotations towards the end of summer; demand rises later. Avoid buying long before installation unless you can store rolls properly, and allow for import and delivery lead times on larger projects.

Frequently asked questions

Why do membrane prices vary so much?

Because quality depends on the bitumen type and quantity, polymer modifier, reinforcement type and weight, real thickness, finish, brand and freight. Two membranes that look the same can contain very different amounts of bitumen and reinforcement.

Is a 4 mm membrane better than a 3 mm one?

More thickness means more bitumen and usually longer life, provided bitumen and reinforcement quality are the same. A good 3 mm polyester-reinforced membrane can outperform a 4 mm membrane with filler-bulked bitumen.

APP or SBS for the Gulf?

For exposed roofs in hot climates, APP membranes are usually preferred for their heat resistance. SBS membranes are more flexible in cold and suit colder areas or structures with more movement. Many projects specify one or the other according to the design.

Is one layer enough?

For most small residential roofs with correct falls, yes. For large, low-slope, industrial and buried applications, a two-layer system is recommended.

How do I know the contractor installs the specified membrane?

Write the exact specification into the contract, check and photograph labels on delivery, and weigh a few rolls if possible. On supervised projects, material inspection requests do this formally.

Is the installed rate based on roof area or membrane area?

It varies. Some contractors measure the roof plan and include upstands in the rate; others measure the actual membrane installed. State the measurement method in the contract.

Are imported membranes better than local ones?

Country of origin alone is not a measure of quality. There are good and poor products from every source. Technical data, a reputable brand, the simple checks above and the installer's experience with the product are better criteria.

How many spare rolls should I buy?

If you have included upstands, details and 10–15 percent waste, one or two spare rolls are usually enough for a residential roof; keep them stored properly for future repairs.

Conclusion

The price of an Isogam membrane, whether per roll or installed, means little without understanding quality and scope. Recognise a good membrane by its label and data, weight, cross-section, flexibility and behaviour under the torch; calculate rolls including upstands, details and waste; and compare installation quotations item by item. The cheapest option is only cheap when it includes every step of proper installation.
