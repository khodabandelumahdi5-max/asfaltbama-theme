A roof leak after waterproofing is one of the most frustrating problems a building owner can face: the roof was waterproofed and paid for, yet the first serious rain brings a new stain on the ceiling. The good news is that in most cases the whole membrane has not failed. Water usually gets in at one or a few specific points: an open lap, a weak upstand on a parapet, a drain outlet, an AC plinth that punctured the membrane, or somewhere that has nothing to do with the membrane at all, such as a crack in the parapet render, a missing coping or a broken downpipe inside a wall.

This guide explains the twelve most common causes of roof leaks after an Isogam or bitumen waterproofing job, with the tell-tale signs of each, shows you step by step how to find the exact point where water enters, describes the correct repair for each cause, and helps you decide whether a local repair is enough or the roof needs to be re-waterproofed. It also covers what to do immediately when a leak appears, how to pursue a warranty claim and how to prevent future leaks, with particular attention to roofs in Iraq and the Gulf.

Key points at a glance

  • Most roof leaks come from details: laps, upstand terminations, drains, pipes and plinths.
  • The stain on the ceiling is not always below the point of entry; water can travel metres under the membrane or inside the slab.
  • A section-by-section water test, working from the bottom up, is the simplest reliable way to locate a leak.
  • Smearing sealant or cold bitumen over a leak is usually only a temporary fix.
  • When leaks are many, scattered and recurring, or the membrane has aged, re-waterproofing is more economical than endless patching.

First things first: what to do when the roof is leaking

  1. Control the electricityIf water is near light fittings, switches, sockets or ceiling wiring, switch off that circuit. Water and electricity are a serious hazard.
  2. Collect the waterPut containers under drips and move valuables, carpets and electrical equipment away.
  3. Check the drainsIf it is safe, check whether a drain is blocked and water is ponding on the roof. After a dust storm, sand and plastic bags often block outlets; clearing one blocked drain can stop a leak immediately.
  4. Temporary coverIf the damage is obvious, such as a clear tear, a heavy polythene sheet weighted down can cover it temporarily. This is not a repair, only a stopgap until the repair crew arrives.
  5. Document itTake dated photographs and videos of the stains, the drip points and the roof. They are valuable both for a warranty claim and for diagnosis.

Warning

Do not go onto the roof in rain, strong wind or darkness. A wet membrane is very slippery, and roofs without parapets carry a fall risk. Leave the detailed inspection until the roof is dry.

Why the stain is not always where the leak is

One of the biggest repair mistakes is patching the membrane directly above the ceiling stain. Once water passes the waterproofing, it follows the path of least resistance:

  • Under the membrane: if the membrane is not fully bonded, water entering at a lap can travel several metres underneath before finding a crack in the slab.
  • Inside the screed to falls: lightweight concrete and aggregate screeds absorb water like a sponge and spread it; water can remain there for months and reach the slab far from where it entered.
  • In rib-and-block slabs: water runs inside the block cavities or along the ribs and may appear at the end of a rib or beside a wall.
  • Along services: water follows electrical conduits, pipes and ducts.

Finding the entry point is a diagnostic task that needs a method, not guesswork.

Twelve common causes of roof leaks after waterproofing

1. Narrow or open laps

Signs: roll edges lift with a spatula or even by hand, there is no continuous bead of bitumen at the lap, or sand and water have got into it.

Cause: laps under 100 mm, too little heat at the lap, bitumen pressed onto foil on foil-faced membranes, or rolls started at the top of the slope so that laps face into the water.

Repair: the lap is cleaned and dried, re-torched and pressed with a hot trowel, and if necessary a cover strip of membrane is bonded over it. If open laps appear all over the roof, the installation method was wrong and a wider rebuild may be needed.

2. Weak upstands and open terminations

Signs: leaks along walls below, especially in wind-driven rain; the top edge of the membrane on the parapet is loose or has separated.

Cause: short upstands, a top edge left unfixed, or no fillet in the corner so the membrane never bonded in the sharp angle and later cracked. Rain running down the wall gets behind the membrane.

Repair: the upstand is re-torched to the wall; if it is too short, an extra strip brings it to 200–300 mm; the top edge is fixed with a termination bar and sealed.

3. Poor detailing at drains

Signs: stains or damp on the ceiling below a drain or on walls along the downpipe route.

Cause: the membrane was not dressed into the outlet and water gets under its edge; the outlet sits higher than the surrounding roof so water ponds around it; or the connection between outlet and downpipe is cracked.

Repair: the drain detail is rebuilt with a reinforcing patch dressed into the outlet, and where necessary the area around the drain is lowered. The downpipe connection must also be checked.

4. Ponding caused by poor falls

Signs: after rain or washing, water stands on parts of the roof for days; silt and algae map the hollows.

Cause: the roof was not laid to proper falls before waterproofing. Standing water keeps laps under constant pressure, deposits silt, and in hot climates accelerates ageing of the bitumen at the pond edges.

Repair: for small hollows, adding a drain at the lowest point may help. For larger ones, the lasting solution is to remove the membrane in that area, correct the falls and re-waterproof.

5. Punctures from equipment and foot traffic

Signs: a sudden leak after AC units, satellite dishes, solar panels or water tanks are installed or serviced.

Cause: thin steel legs sinking into the membrane in summer heat, mounts screwed through the membrane into the slab, dropped sharp tools, or equipment dragged across the roof.

Repair: the puncture is cleaned and patched; equipment is set on plinths or wide load-spreading bases, and every mount fixed to the slab gets its own flashing.

6. Blisters under the membrane

Signs: raised areas that feel soft underfoot or sound hollow, sometimes cracked on top.

Cause: moisture or air trapped under the membrane because it was laid on a damp deck, on wet primer or with too little heat. As the roof heats up during the day, vapour expands and the blister grows. A blister does not always leak, but once its surface cracks, it lets water in.

Repair: the blister is cut in a cross, the substrate dried, the flaps re-bonded with heat and a patch applied extending at least 100 mm beyond the cut.

7. Cracks from structural movement

Signs: long, straight cracks in the membrane, often along expansion joints, junctions between two slabs or over cracks in the slab.

Cause: the membrane was laid tight across an expansion joint, or the slab has active cracks that open and close with temperature.

Repair: expansion joints need a dedicated flexible detail. Active structural cracks must be assessed by an engineer; a surface patch over them will not last.

8. Ageing of the membrane

Signs: foil has peeled off large areas, the bitumen beneath shows networked cracks, reinforcement fibres are exposed and the membrane is brittle.

Cause: the membrane has reached the end of its life, the materials were poor, or an unfaced membrane was left for years without a reflective coating under intense sun.

Repair: patching only buys time. Re-waterproofing with proper preparation is usually the sensible choice.

9. Stair enclosures, roof doors and enclosure roofs

Signs: leaks in the top stairwell or beside the roof door.

Cause: the membrane does not pass under the door threshold, enclosure walls have short upstands, or the enclosure roof is not waterproofed and sheds water onto its own walls.

Repair: the threshold is rebuilt so the waterproofing passes under the frame and up the wall, and the enclosure roof is waterproofed and drained to a gutter.

10. AC ducts, condensate and roof openings

Signs: leaks around ceiling diffusers or duct openings on the top floor, sometimes even without rain.

Cause: ducts pass through the roof without a raised kerb and flashing, or condensate from AC units runs across the roof and keeps the area around the duct permanently wet. In the Gulf, where AC units run most of the year, condensate is a constant source of water on the roof.

Repair: ducts are set on kerbs higher than the roof surface with the membrane turned up them, and condensate is piped to a drain.

11. Water entering somewhere other than the roof

A significant share of the "roof leaks" we inspect have nothing to do with the membrane:

  • Cracked parapet render or open joints at the top of the wall that carry water behind the waterproofing or into the wall.
  • Missing or poorly sloped copings that let rain soak straight into the parapet.
  • Cracked downpipes inside walls or ceilings.
  • Plumbing: top-floor water supply, rooftop tank connections and overflows.
  • Window heads and frames on the top floor that leak in wind-driven rain.
  • Condensation: cold air-conditioned ceilings under a hot, humid roof, or cold ceilings in winter, can produce damp patches that look like leaks, especially where roof insulation is poor.

Ruling these out before touching the membrane avoids wasted money.

12. Dust storms, blocked drains and intense downpours

Signs: leaks that appear only in heavy rain, often after a dust storm, sometimes with water standing high against the parapets.

Cause: sand, dust and debris block the drain outlets, and a short intense downpour fills the roof faster than the remaining drains can empty it. Water rises above the height of the upstands or pushes under laps near the parapets. In colder regions, such as parts of the Kurdistan Region, snow piled against walls and frozen drains cause the same effect.

Repair and prevention: keep drains and gratings clear, especially before the rainy season and after dust storms, check that drain capacity is adequate, and make sure upstands are high enough. Where drains are too few, add overflow outlets through the parapet.

How water travels in different roof structures

Rib-and-block slabs

Common in residential buildings across the region. Water passing the waterproofing and screed enters the topping concrete and drops through cracks or joints between blocks and ribs, often travelling along the ribs. Parallel, linear stains on the ceiling usually indicate this path.

Solid concrete slabs

Water passes through slab cracks, around pipe penetrations and at construction joints. Stains tend to be closer to the entry point, although water can still travel under the membrane or in the screed.

Metal roofs and warehouses

On sheet roofs, leaks typically come from fasteners, side and end laps, internal gutters and rooflights, and water often runs along the sheet or purlins. Diagnosis and repair follow different rules from concrete roofs.

Leaks on inverted roofs and sprayed foam roofs

Two roof types common in the Gulf need a slightly different approach.

Inverted roofs

On an inverted roof, the waterproofing sits under insulation boards, a filter layer and gravel or pavers, so it cannot be inspected directly. Leaks are diagnosed by first checking everything that is visible: drains and their gratings, parapet upstands and terminations above the ballast, penetrations and thresholds. If those are sound, the ballast and boards are lifted in the suspect area, starting near drains and upstands, the membrane is inspected and tested, and then everything is reinstated in the correct order. Boards and ballast must be put back carefully so they do not float or shift in the next downpour.

Sprayed polyurethane foam roofs

On foam roofs, leaks often start where the protective coating has worn, cracked or been damaged, exposing the foam to sun; the foam then degrades, cracks and lets water in. Bird damage, foot traffic and equipment installation are other common causes. Repairs involve cutting out damaged foam, re-foaming or filling the area and restoring the protective coating over a generous area. Where the coating has failed widely, recoating the whole roof is part of the solution.

Rooftop water tanks and solar panels

Rooftop tanks leak in two ways: through their own overflow and connections, which can look exactly like a roof leak, and through damage to the waterproofing under their stands. Check the tank, its float valve, overflow and pipe connections before blaming the membrane. For solar arrays, inspect every mount that is fixed into the slab, the cable penetrations and the areas under the panels where sand collects and drains may be obstructed.

Why leaks show up with the first rain of the season

Through the long, hot summer, sun and heat age the membrane surface, dust and sand accumulate in drains, and fine cracks open with the daily temperature cycle. The first heavy rain of autumn or winter then finds blocked outlets and open cracks at the same time. That is why the best time for inspection and preventive repair is at the end of summer, before the rainy season, when the weather is dry and there is time to fix problems.

How to locate the leak

Step 1: Gather information from inside

  • Mark the stains on a plan or sketch and note their distance from the walls.
  • Note when the leak appears: immediately after rain starts, hours later, only in heavy rain, only in wind-driven rain from one direction, or even without rain.
  • A leak without rain usually points to plumbing, AC condensate or condensation, not the roof.
  • A leak only in wind-driven rain often points to walls, edges or windows.

Step 2: Visual inspection of the roof

  • Laps with lifting edges or sand inside.
  • Upstand terminations on parapets and stair enclosures.
  • Drain outlets, gratings and leaf guards.
  • Around pipes, vents, ducts and plinths.
  • Blisters, cracks, tears and scorched areas.
  • Signs of ponding and silt.
  • Cracks in parapet render and the condition of copings.

Step 3: Section-by-section water test

  1. Start at the bottomHose the lowest areas first, such as around drains. If you start at the top, water runs down and you cannot tell which section leaked.
  2. One section at a timeFor example, only around one drain, only one parapet or only one plinth.
  3. Be patientWet each section for 15 to 30 minutes while someone watches inside below the stain. Water sometimes appears with a delay.
  4. Move up section by sectionIf nothing appears, move on: laps, then parapets, then copings and facades.
  5. Record everythingNote each section and its result so the conclusion is reliable.

Step 4: Specialist tools

In difficult cases, a specialist may use a moisture meter to map wet areas under the membrane or in the slab, a thermal camera to see damp zones with a different temperature, or a full flood test. These are particularly useful when water has spread under the membrane or through the screed.

An illustrative example

To show the method, here is a typical situation. It is a teaching example, not a report on a specific project.

In a five-storey building, the top-floor flat gets a stain beside a bedroom wall after heavy rain. The roof was waterproofed two years ago, and the owner has already had the membrane above the stain patched twice, without success.

  1. Information from insideThe stain appears only in rain driven by wind from one direction. That suggests water is not entering through the flat roof surface but through a wall or edge.
  2. Visual inspectionThe membrane above the stain is sound. But the parapet on that side has cracked render at the top, no coping, and a short length where the upstand has separated.
  3. Water testThe roof surface beside the wall is tested first: nothing after 30 minutes. Then the upstand: nothing. Finally, the top of the parapet and its render are wetted, and after about 20 minutes the stain appears inside.
  4. ConclusionWater enters through the top of the parapet and the cracked render, runs down inside the wall behind the upstand and reaches the slab. The earlier patches were useless.
  5. RepairThe render cracks are repaired, a sloped coping is installed, the separated upstand is re-bonded and fixed, and the area is water-tested again.

This is why diagnosis by method is cheaper than patching by guesswork.

How a proper patch is made

  1. Mark the areaThe damage plus at least 150–200 mm all round.
  2. Remove loose materialRotten, blistered or debonded membrane is cut back to sound, bonded material.
  3. Clean and drySand, silt and moisture are removed; if the substrate is wet, wait for it to dry.
  4. Prepare the surfaceOn foil-faced membranes, the foil around the repair is burned off or abraded so the patch bonds to bitumen, not to foil. Primer goes on any exposed substrate.
  5. Apply the patchThe membrane patch is fully torched and its edges pressed with a hot trowel until a continuous bead of bitumen appears.
  6. ProtectOn unfaced membranes, a reflective coating is applied over the patch.
  7. TestThe patch is water-tested.

What about sealants and cold bitumen?

Silicone, general-purpose sealants, cold bitumen or mastic smeared over a leak may hold water back for a few weeks, but they bond poorly to wet, sandy membranes, crack in the sun and sometimes hide the real entry point. They are acceptable in an emergency but are no substitute for a proper patch.

After the repair: dealing with a wet ceiling

  • Allow time: screeds and slabs hold water and may take weeks or months to dry. A faint stain after a repair is not always a new leak; residual water may still be coming out.
  • Ventilate: ventilate the room below so moisture leaves faster and mould does not grow. In humid coastal cities, a dehumidifier helps.
  • Wait before repainting: painting a damp ceiling leads to peeling and new stains. Wait until the plaster is fully dry, scrape off blistered plaster, repair and use a suitable sealer primer.
  • Treat mould: clean and disinfect mouldy surfaces; mould is a health risk, particularly for children and people with allergies.
  • Check electrics: if water reached wiring or light fittings, have an electrician check them before reconnecting.
  • Check the structure: on older slabs, if the leak has been long-standing, have the condition of the reinforcement assessed.

Tiled roofs and roofs with a screed

  • First check tile joints, floor drains, door thresholds and the junction between tiles and walls; water sometimes gets above the upstand and behind the tiles.
  • Section-by-section water testing works here too.
  • Repair usually means lifting tiles in the suspect area, repairing the waterproofing, testing and re-laying the tiles.
  • If leaks are widespread, stripping all tiles and re-waterproofing may be more economical.

This is why we always insist on a flood test before tiling.

Leaks on new buildings

  • Construction water: screeds laid in rain or soaked before waterproofing hold water and may release damp into the slab for some time after waterproofing, sometimes causing blisters.
  • Damage by following trades: installing services, AC units, balustrades and facade elements after waterproofing often damages it.
  • Unfinished details: upstands meant to be fixed after facade work and then forgotten, or drains not yet connected to downpipes.

On new buildings, the developer and waterproofing contractor are usually responsible for rectifying defects during the defects liability period; contact them before any repair and keep records.

Does the warranty cover the leak?

  • Read the warranty: it usually covers leaks caused by defective workmanship, but not damage from later equipment installation, foot traffic, improper cleaning or alterations by others.
  • Prepare documentation: photographs, the date the leak appeared and the handover record.
  • Don't repair it yourself first: repairs by a third party may void the warranty.
  • Notify in writing: send a dated message or letter.

What affects the cost of repair?

  • Diagnosis time: obvious leaks are quick; hidden leaks need testing and more time.
  • Type and extent of repair: a small patch, rebuilding one upstand, or correcting falls in an area.
  • Access: building height and how materials reach the roof.
  • Equipment on the roof: whether AC units or tanks must be moved.
  • Finishes: tiles or screed over the membrane must be lifted and relaid.

A timely repair almost always costs far less than the damage a continuing leak does to the slab, finishes and contents.

When you need a specialist

  • The leak has returned after several repairs.
  • Stains appear in several places far apart.
  • The membrane is under tiles or a screed.
  • The roof is large, industrial or metal.
  • The leak appears with no clear link to rain.
  • The building is old and there are signs of corroded reinforcement or falling plaster.
  • There is a dispute with neighbours or the original contractor and a technical report is needed.

Questions to ask the repair contractor

  1. How did you diagnose the cause? Will you water-test?
  2. Exactly which part will you repair, and why?
  3. Will you remove the loose material or patch over it?
  4. Will the patch bond to bitumen or to foil?
  5. Will you test the repair afterwards?
  6. Do you give a written warranty on the repair?
  7. If the cause is not the membrane (render, coping, pipes), will you check those too?

A contractor who prices "the stain" without an inspection and a test is unlikely to solve the problem.

Prevention

  • Inspect the roof twice a year, before and after the rainy season, and after every major dust storm.
  • Keep drains, gratings and leaf guards clear.
  • Sweep sand and silt off the roof.
  • Repair lifting edges and suspect laps early; early repairs are cheap.
  • Recoat unfaced membranes with a reflective coating every few years.
  • Do not let AC, satellite or solar installers drill or fix mounts without coordination.
  • Install new equipment on plinths or wide bases with proper flashings.
  • Pipe AC condensate and tank overflows to drains.
  • Check parapet copings and render during inspections.

Common mistakes when repairing leaks

  1. Patching above the ceiling stain without finding the real entry point.
  2. Smearing sealant or cold bitumen over a wet, sandy membrane.
  3. Laying a new membrane over the whole roof on top of wet old layers without fixing the cause.
  4. Ignoring walls, copings, render and plumbing as possible sources.
  5. Repairing in the rain or on a wet surface.
  6. A patch bonded to foil instead of bitumen.
  7. Repairs by a third party during the warranty period without informing the original contractor.
  8. Delaying the repair while water damages the slab every day.

Frequently asked questions

Why is there still a leak after new waterproofing?

The most common reasons are poor detailing at parapets, drains and pipes, narrow laps, installation on a wet substrate and ponding. Sometimes the water is not coming through the roof at all but through render, copings or pipes. Water held in the screed from before waterproofing can also cause damp for some time afterwards.

Can I seal the leak with sealant or bitumen?

As an emergency measure, yes, but it will not last. A proper repair removes loose material, dries and prepares the surface and torches on a membrane patch.

When is the best time to repair a membrane?

On a dry day after the roof has dried completely. In the Gulf summer, repairs are done in the cooler hours. A proper repair cannot be made in rain or on a wet surface.

The stain has dried. Does that mean the leak is fixed?

Not necessarily; it may simply have stopped raining. Until the cause is found and repaired, the stain will return with the next rain. A water test confirms whether the leak is fixed.

How can I tell a leak from condensation?

A leak usually appears or grows after rain. Condensation appears on cold surfaces, in humid rooms or under poorly insulated roofs, often in corners and on external walls, and has no link to rainfall. Roof insulation and ventilation reduce condensation.

Can the leak come from the neighbouring building?

Yes. With adjoining buildings, water from a higher neighbouring roof or the gap between buildings can enter the shared wall and appear in your ceiling or wall. The separation joint and the neighbour's roof edge must be checked, and repairs coordinated.

Is a liquid coating suitable for repairing a leaking membrane?

Some reinforced liquid systems can repair complex details on a sound membrane, provided they are compatible with bitumen and applied on a clean, dry surface at the right thickness. If the membrane below is rotten or debonded, a liquid overcoat will not solve the problem.

How long does a leak repair take?

Local repairs usually take a day; difficult diagnosis, correcting falls or replacing large areas take longer.

Who is responsible for a roof leak in an apartment building?

In most apartment buildings the roof is common property, so waterproofing repairs are organised by the owners' association or building management and shared, unless a particular unit caused the damage, for example by installing equipment. A technical inspection that identifies the cause before any dispute saves time and money.

What if the leak happens in winter and a proper repair is not possible?

Keep drains clear, cover any obvious damage with a weighted sheet, collect water inside, and carry out the proper repair on the first dry days. In the region, dry spells between rains are usually frequent enough to schedule repairs quickly.

Can a leak damage the structure?

A long-standing leak can corrode reinforcement and steel beams, particularly in older buildings and in coastal cities where chlorides accelerate corrosion. Signs such as rust stains, cracking along ribs or falling plaster should be assessed by an engineer.

Conclusion

A roof leak after waterproofing usually comes from a specific point and can be repaired once it is correctly diagnosed. The key is to find the real entry point by method rather than patching above the ceiling stain: gather information from inside, inspect visually, water-test section by section from the bottom up, and use specialist tools where needed. The choice between a local repair and re-waterproofing then depends on the overall condition of the membrane and the root causes.
