A modified bitumen membrane, known across Iran and much of the region simply as Isogam, is one of the most widely used ways to waterproof a flat roof. Installed properly, an Isogam roof membrane stays watertight for many years, even under the fierce summer sun of Iraq and the Gulf. In our experience on hundreds of residential, commercial and industrial roofs, most leaks that appear in the first year or two are not caused by the membrane itself but by poor installation: a surface that was not cleaned, a fall that does not carry water to the drains, laps that are too narrow, too little heat, or upstands and details that were rushed.

This guide walks through the complete Isogam roof membrane installation process, step by step, from the first site visit to the flood test and handover. Whether you are a building owner, a project manager, a consultant or a main contractor supervising a waterproofing subcontractor, you will know exactly what should happen at each stage and what to check before you sign off the work.

Key points at a glance

  • The roof deck must be dry, clean, sound and laid to falls towards the drains. A membrane hides defects in the substrate; it does not fix them.
  • A coat of bitumen primer is applied first and must be completely dry before torching.
  • Rolls start at the lowest point of the roof and are lapped by at least 100 mm.
  • Internal corners receive a mortar fillet, and the membrane is turned up the parapet by 200–300 mm and mechanically fixed.
  • The roof is accepted after a 24–48 hour flood test, not after a visual inspection alone.

What is an Isogam membrane?

"Isogam" began as a brand name and is now the everyday word, in Farsi and in the Arabic spoken across Iraq and the Gulf (الإيزوجام), for prefabricated modified bitumen waterproofing membranes. The membrane is supplied in rolls, most commonly one metre wide and ten metres long, in thicknesses of 3 mm and 4 mm. Each roll is a composite of several layers, and understanding them helps you choose the right product for the roof.

Modified bitumen

The body of the membrane is bitumen modified with polymers so that it does not soften and flow in the heat or crack in the cold. The two common families are APP (atactic polypropylene) and SBS (styrene-butadiene-styrene). APP membranes have better resistance to high temperatures and ultraviolet light and are the usual choice for exposed roofs in hot climates such as southern Iraq, Kuwait, Bahrain, Oman and the UAE. SBS membranes are more flexible at low temperatures and suit colder, mountainous areas and structures with more movement, such as parts of the Kurdistan Region in winter.

Reinforcement

Inside the bitumen sits a reinforcement layer that gives the membrane its tensile strength and dimensional stability. Polyester reinforcement has higher tensile and tear strength and tolerates movement better; glass fibre gives good dimensional stability but is more brittle. Cheaper products sometimes use weaker carriers. For roofs with foot traffic, large roofs and industrial buildings, a polyester-reinforced membrane is the safer choice.

Surface finish

The upper face of the membrane usually carries one of these finishes:

  • Aluminium foil: reflects sunlight, keeps the surface cooler and shields the bitumen from UV. It is the most common finish for exposed roofs and is especially useful where roof surface temperatures exceed 70 °C in summer.
  • Mineral granules or sand: give a tougher, more abrasion-resistant surface for roofs with light traffic or roofs that will receive another finish.
  • Plain or polyethylene film: for membranes that will be covered, for example under tiles, screeds, landscaping or foundations.

Which membrane for which roof?

Before you think about installation, make sure the membrane type suits the roof. Even the best workmanship cannot make up for the wrong material. The table below is a general guide; every project should be matched to its actual conditions and specification.

Greater thickness does not automatically mean better quality. The quality of the bitumen, the type of reinforcement and the consistency of manufacture matter more. A membrane whose bitumen has been bulked out with fillers will crack early even if it measures 4 mm.

Before work starts: survey, quantities and planning

Good waterproofing starts on the day of the survey, not on the day the rolls arrive. During the first visit, the contractor should inspect and record:

  • The existing waterproofing: is there an old membrane or hot bitumen layer? Is it sound and fully bonded, or brittle, blistered and delaminating?
  • Falls and ponding: does water stand on the roof after rain or after washing? Dried water marks and silt deposits reveal low spots.
  • Drains: their size, number, condition and spacing, and whether each drain really is the lowest point of its catchment.
  • Roof-mounted equipment: air-conditioning units, ducts, water tanks, solar panels, antennas, gas pipes, flues and vents. Every one of them is a detail that has to be sealed.
  • Parapets and edges: parapet height, the condition of the wall finishes, and how the membrane will terminate at the edges.
  • Existing leaks: stains on the ceilings below. A stain is not always directly beneath the point where water enters, but it is an important clue.

Calculating the area and number of rolls

The membrane area is the plan area of the roof plus the upstands on the parapets and around equipment. For example, a roof of 10 × 12 m has a plan area of 120 m². If the membrane is turned up 300 mm on the parapets, the 44 m perimeter adds about 13 m². Then add an allowance for laps and cutting waste, typically 10 to 15 percent. That is why the number of rolls is never simply the roof area divided by ten.

Programme

A typical residential roof can usually be completed by an experienced team in one or two working days. If the old waterproofing must be stripped, the falls corrected or fillets formed, allow extra time for mortar and primer to dry. Rushing at this point, torching onto a screed or primer that is still damp, is one of the main causes of blisters and delamination later.

Step 1: Strip the old waterproofing (where needed)

The first real decision is whether the new membrane can go over the existing waterproofing or whether the old layers must be removed. Torching a new membrane onto an old layer that is brittle, blistered, delaminated or wet means bonding new material to something that is itself no longer bonded to the roof. Any movement of the old layer will drag the new one with it, and moisture trapped between the two will form larger and larger blisters.

As a rule, if the old waterproofing is sound, dry, flat and fully bonded, it can sometimes be cleaned, its blisters cut and repaired, primed and overlaid. If several layers have built up over the years, large areas have lifted or water is trapped underneath, complete removal is the only sound option.

Watch the roof load

Every additional layer of membrane and screed adds dead load to the slab. On older buildings that have been re-waterproofed several times, stripping the old layers improves the waterproofing and also removes weight from the structure.

Step 2: Clean and repair the deck

Before anything else, the roof must be thoroughly cleaned: soil, gravel, debris, loose bitumen, old membrane offcuts, leaves and anything sharp. A pebble left under the membrane acts like a small nail; with foot traffic and daily temperature swings it gradually punctures the sheet. The surface is swept and then blown or brushed free of dust. In the Gulf and Iraq, where dust storms are frequent, the surface may need cleaning again immediately before priming.

Then come the repairs:

  • Mortar and concrete ridges are ground off to leave a flat surface.
  • Cracks and holes are filled with mortar. Wide or active cracks may indicate structural movement and should be assessed separately.
  • Sharp arrises on walls and steps are rounded so they do not cut the membrane.
  • Moisture is checked. After rain or washing, the deck must be allowed to dry completely.

A simple moisture test: tape a clear polythene sheet about 500 × 500 mm to the deck overnight, sealing all four edges. If droplets or darkening appear under the sheet in the morning, the deck still holds moisture and is not ready.

Step 3: Lay the roof to falls

Ponding water is the biggest enemy of any roof membrane. Water that stands for days adds load to the slab, leaves silt on the surface every time it evaporates and, in cold regions, puts laps under stress when it freezes. In hot climates, standing water combined with intense sun also accelerates ageing of the bitumen along the edges of the ponds. No membrane can replace correct falls.

For flat roofs, a fall of about 1.5 to 2 percent (15–20 mm per metre) towards the drains is normally enough. Falls are formed with lightweight concrete, lightweight aggregate with a topping, or sand-cement screed. Key points:

  • Falls must be set out with a level, string lines and a straightedge. Screeds laid "by eye" almost always leave small hollows.
  • Each drain must be the lowest point of its own catchment; large roofs are divided into several catchments.
  • After the screed is laid, carry out a simple water test: pour a few buckets of water and see where it stands. Correcting a hollow now is cheap; after the membrane is laid it is costly and difficult.
  • The screed must be fully cured and dry before priming.

Step 4: Form fillets at internal corners

At the 90-degree junction between the roof and a wall, the membrane has to make a sharp bend. At that point it either fails to bond fully, leaving a small air channel behind it, or it cracks under tension and temperature cycling. The solution is a fillet: a triangular or rounded strip of mortar in the corner that turns the sharp angle into a sloping face of about 45 degrees or a gentle curve.

A fillet is typically 50 to 100 mm on each face and runs along every parapet, at the base of stair enclosures and around large ducts and plinths. This small detail, often skipped on rushed jobs, is one of the most effective ways to prevent leaks at the edges.

Step 5: Apply bitumen primer

Bitumen primer is a thin, solvent-based bitumen applied by brush, roller or spray. It penetrates the pores of the screed or concrete, binds residual dust and leaves a bituminous surface to which the membrane bonds far better. Torching a membrane without primer, especially onto a dusty screed, dramatically reduces adhesion.

  • Primer goes on a clean, dry surface. On a damp surface it forms a weak skin instead of penetrating.
  • The coat must be even, without puddles. Puddles dry slowly and cause blisters under the membrane.
  • Coverage and any thinning must follow the manufacturer's instructions and depend on how absorbent the surface is.
  • The membrane is laid only when the primer is completely dry. Primer that still contains solvent can ignite under the torch, and trapped solvent later forms blisters. Drying time depends on temperature and humidity and is usually a few hours; in the Gulf summer it can be short, in a humid coastal winter much longer.

Primer is also applied to the parts of parapets and equipment plinths that the membrane will be turned up onto.

Step 6: Set out the rolls and laps

Before torching, the rolls are unrolled and laid out dry to establish the line of each roll and the position of laps and cuts. This dry lay-out prevents mistakes that cannot be corrected once the sheet is bonded. The main rules:

  1. Start at the lowest pointThe first roll is laid along the drain line or lowest edge, and subsequent rows move up the slope. Like roof tiles, each row overlaps the one below, so water runs over the laps rather than into them.
  2. Adequate lapsSide laps are at least 100 mm and end laps usually 100–150 mm. Most membranes have a printed guide line or a selvedge strip that marks the lap width.
  3. Stagger the end lapsEnd laps in adjacent rows should be offset by around one metre so that four thicknesses never meet at a single point.
  4. Lap directionThe upper edge of every lap must face down the slope. A lap that opens up the slope channels water into itself.
  5. Keep laps away from critical pointsArrange the lay-out so that laps do not fall directly over a drain outlet or in a corner.

Step 7: Torch-apply the membrane

In the standard torch-on method, the underside of the roll is heated with a gas torch until its bitumen melts; the roll is then gradually unrolled and pressed onto the primed deck. The quality of this step depends more on the skill of the operator than on anything else.

What does the right amount of heat look like?

The flame should heat both the underside of the roll and the deck at the same time, sweeping back and forth across the full width of the roll. The sign of correct heating is a glossy, evenly molten surface across the whole width and a narrow bead of bitumen, about 5 to 10 mm, squeezing out at the lap as the roll is pressed down.

Too little heat

  • The bitumen only softens on the surface and does not fuse with the primer.
  • Air is trapped under the sheet and later forms blisters.
  • Laps can be opened by hand or lifted by wind.

Too much heat

  • The bitumen burns, gives off black smoke and loses its polymer properties.
  • The reinforcement is damaged and the membrane becomes brittle.
  • The foil wrinkles and darkens.

Sealing the laps

Laps are the most sensitive part of every roll. After bonding, each lap is pressed and smoothed with a hot trowel or heated steel spatula so that the bitumen of the two sheets fuses into one continuous seam. On foil-faced membranes, the lap zone must be free of foil, either by design or by burning the foil off before lapping; bitumen pressed onto foil does not create a true bond.

A simple check during the work: the supervisor runs the tip of a spatula along the edge of each lap. If it slides under the edge easily or the edge lifts, the lap must be reheated and pressed again.

Step 8: Upstands and details

More than half the leaks we are called to investigate come from details, not from the middle of the roof. A detail is anywhere the flat surface is interrupted: a wall, a drain, a pipe, a plinth or an edge. Details take time, and in very low-priced quotations they are the first place where corners are cut.

Parapet upstands

The membrane is turned up the parapet by at least 200–300 mm, more where ponding is possible. Crucially, the top edge of the upstand must never be left free. If it is, rainwater running down the wall gets behind the membrane and finds its way under the whole roof. The top edge is fixed with a metal termination bar and sealed, or tucked into a chase cut in the wall and the chase sealed. A capping or coping over the parapet adds further protection.

Drains

The membrane must be dressed into the drain outlet and bonded to its sides so that water flows off the membrane directly into the drain and has no path under the edge of the sheet. A reinforcing patch of membrane is usually laid around the outlet before the main roll. The area around each drain should sit slightly lower than the rest of the roof so that water is drawn towards it. A leaf guard or grating prevents blockage by leaves, sand and plastic.

Pipes, vents and ducts

Every penetration receives its own membrane collar that runs up the pipe and out onto the roof. A clamp or sealed sleeve prevents the top of the collar from opening. Hot flues from boilers or heaters need a safe clearance and heat-resistant materials; the membrane must never be bonded directly to a hot surface.

Air-conditioning units and plant

Split and package air-conditioning units, water tanks and other heavy plant should stand on plinths formed before waterproofing, with fillets around them; the membrane then runs up the plinth. Placing equipment directly on the membrane, especially on thin steel legs, is a common cause of failure in hot climates: in summer the legs sink into the softened bitumen and puncture it. Condensate from AC units should be piped to a drain rather than left to run across the roof.

Movement joints and free edges

Where the structure has an expansion joint, the membrane must not simply run across it like the rest of the roof. A dedicated detail with a flexible loop or separate strip is needed so that structural movement does not tear the sheet. On roofs without a parapet, the membrane runs over the edge and down the face of the fascia and is finished with a metal drip or edge trim so that water drops clear of the building instead of getting behind the membrane.

Step 9: A second layer for large and industrial roofs

For most small residential roofs, a single layer of good-quality membrane, properly installed, is sufficient. On large roofs, low-slope industrial roofs, roofs over sensitive spaces such as warehouses, data or equipment rooms, and roofs where the waterproofing will be buried under another finish, a two-layer system is recommended. The cap sheet is laid with a half-roll offset relative to the base sheet so that no lap sits over another. The base sheet is usually unfaced and the cap sheet foil-faced or granule-surfaced.

On factories, warehouses and logistics yards, roof waterproofing is often planned together with asphalt paving of the yard and access roads, so that both packages run to one programme without stopping operations.

Step 10: Protect the membrane surface

Ultraviolet radiation and large daily temperature swings gradually oxidise bitumen and make it brittle. In Iraq and the Gulf, where roof surfaces can reach extreme temperatures in summer, this effect is stronger than in temperate climates. A foil facing reduces it considerably. If the membrane is unfaced, it must be protected with a solar-reflective aluminium coating or another suitable protective finish, and the coating must be designed for bitumen, since some paints react with it and crack.

If the roof will carry regular foot traffic or be used as a terrace, a separation layer, then a screed and tiles or another wearing surface, are laid over the membrane. The buried membrane is then protected from sun and impact and lasts much longer; the trade-off is that repairs are harder to reach, so the quality of the membrane installation becomes even more important.

Step 11: Flood test and handover

The simplest and most reliable quality check is a flood test. The drain outlets are temporarily plugged and a shallow layer of water, usually a few centimetres, is held on the roof for 24 to 48 hours. During that time the soffit below is inspected for damp and stains, especially beneath drains, corners and penetrations.

  • The water depth must stay below the height of the upstands.
  • Before testing, confirm that the slab can carry the load. On weak or old slabs, test in sections and with a shallow depth.
  • After the test, unplug the drains and confirm free flow.

If a flood test is not possible, at least carry out a hose test on the critical details. Record the test, its date and its result in the handover certificate; it is the document your warranty will refer to.

Weather conditions for installation

Membranes can be installed in any season, provided the conditions on the day are right:

  • Do not install in rain, fog or on a wet deck. Even morning dew, common on the Gulf coast, must dry off before work starts.
  • Severe cold reduces adhesion and makes the membrane brittle; unrolling a cold roll can crack it. In cold weather, work in the warmest hours and keep rolls somewhere warmer before use.
  • Extreme heat is also a problem: the membrane softens and footprints and deformation remain wherever people walk. In the Gulf summer, crews often work in the early morning and late afternoon and keep traffic off freshly laid sheets. Rolls must be stored in the shade, as a roll left in full sun can stick to itself.
  • High winds and dust storms make the flame hard to control, blow dust onto the primer and increase the fire risk. Work should stop until conditions improve.

Twelve common installation mistakes

  1. Torching onto a wet deck or fresh screed.
  2. Omitting the primer, or torching onto primer that is still wet.
  3. Starting at the top of the slope and creating laps that face into the water.
  4. Laps narrower than 100 mm to save material.
  5. End laps aligned in adjacent rows.
  6. Too little heat, so the sheet is bonded in spots rather than fully.
  7. Burning the membrane with too much heat.
  8. No fillets, with the membrane forced into a sharp corner.
  9. Short upstands with the top edge left loose.
  10. Careless detailing at drains and pipes.
  11. Heavy plant placed directly on the membrane.
  12. Accepting the roof without a flood test.

Handover checklist for owners and supervisors

On the day of handover, check these points on the roof yourself. If any answer is no, ask the contractor to correct it before final payment:

  • The surface is free of blisters, wrinkles and ridges, and does not sound hollow underfoot.
  • Every lap shows a continuous bead of bitumen, and edges do not lift when tested with a spatula.
  • End laps in adjacent rows are staggered.
  • Upstands are at least 200–300 mm high, and their top edge is fixed and sealed.
  • The membrane is dressed into every drain outlet, and all drains are open and clean.
  • Every pipe, vent and plinth has its own collar or flashing.
  • No burnt foil or torn membrane is visible anywhere.
  • After a water test, no water remains on the roof and it all runs to the drains.
  • A flood test has been carried out and its result recorded.
  • Offcuts, debris and gas cylinders have been removed from the roof.
  • The brand, type and thickness of membrane match the contract.
  • A written warranty stating the contractor's obligations has been issued.

Annual maintenance

A finished membrane is not maintenance-free. A few simple tasks each year noticeably extend its life:

  • Inspect the roof twice a year, before the rainy season and after it.
  • Clear drains and gratings of leaves, sand and plastic.
  • Sweep off sand and silt. Dust storms leave deposits that hold moisture and allow plants to take root.
  • Check laps and upstands; repair any edge that has started to lift before it lets water in.
  • Renew the reflective coating on unfaced membranes every few years.
  • Brief AC technicians and other trades not to put sharp tools on the membrane or move plant without protection.
  • Where snow falls, clear it carefully with plastic tools; a steel shovel will tear the membrane.

What affects the cost of an Isogam roof?

The installed cost does not depend on the price of the rolls alone, which is why quotations can differ so much. The factors that decide the real cost of a roof are:

  • Membrane type, brand and thickness, and whether the system is single or two-layer.
  • Stripping old waterproofing and disposing of it, which on some roofs takes longer than laying the new membrane.
  • Correcting falls and repairing the deck, especially on roofs with many hollows.
  • The number of details: drains, pipes, plinths and the length of parapets. A small, busy roof may cost more per square metre than a large, simple one.
  • Access: building height, how rolls are lifted and any restrictions on working hours.
  • Total area: on very small roofs, mobilisation costs take a larger share.
  • Surface protection: reflective coating, separation layer or tiles.

When comparing two quotations, check which one includes primer, fillets, full detailing, a flood test and a written warranty. A quotation that leaves these out usually looks cheaper but simply transfers the cost of future repairs to the owner.

Isogam membrane or hot bitumen and jute?

Hot bitumen and jute (hessian) roofing, known in Farsi as qirguni, is the older, built-up method: several coats of hot bitumen with layers of jute fabric laid in place. It is still used for foundations, under floor finishes and on some structures, and it adapts well to irregular surfaces and complex details. For most exposed roofs today, a modified bitumen membrane is quicker to install, more consistent in quality and leaves a cleaner site. Our team installs both systems, and the choice depends on the structure, the substrate and where the waterproofing will be.

Torch safety on the roof

Torch-on work is done with an open flame at height and carries two main risks: fire and falls. These precautions matter to owners and main contractors too, because an accident happens on their site:

  • Gas cylinders stand upright, in the shade and away from the flame; hoses and regulators are checked before work.
  • A fire extinguisher is kept ready on the roof.
  • Combustible materials such as foam insulation, timber, plastics and rubbish are cleared from the work area; special care is taken at duct openings and vents where flame can be drawn inside.
  • Primer is fully dry, as solvent from wet primer is flammable.
  • At unprotected edges, workers use fall protection.
  • At the end of each day, the work area is checked for smouldering material.

Tip for owners and main contractors

Before work starts, ask the waterproofing contractor exactly which membrane brand and thickness will be used, whether primer and fillets are included, how high the upstands will be and whether a flood test will be carried out. The answers to those four questions reveal the difference between a sound proposal and a risky low price.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to install an Isogam roof membrane?

On a typical residential roof that needs little preparation, installation usually takes one to two working days. If the old waterproofing must be removed or falls and fillets formed, the drying time of screeds and primer is added to the programme.

Can the membrane be installed without primer?

It is not recommended. Primer binds surface dust and greatly improves adhesion. Leaving it out is a common reason for membranes lifting and blistering.

How wide should the laps be?

Side laps should be at least 100 mm and end laps usually 100–150 mm. End laps in adjacent rows should be staggered rather than aligned.

How high should the membrane go up the parapet?

At least 200–300 mm, with the top edge mechanically fixed with a termination bar or tucked into a chase and sealed, so water cannot get behind it.

Can Isogam be installed in the Gulf summer heat?

Yes, but crews usually work in the cooler hours, store rolls in the shade and keep foot traffic off freshly laid membrane, which softens in extreme heat. Primer dries quickly in summer, but the deck must still be free of dust.

Why do blisters appear after installation?

Blisters usually come from moisture or air trapped under the membrane: a wet deck or screed, primer that was not dry, or too little heat during bonding. Small, stable blisters are not always a problem, but large or growing blisters should be investigated and repaired.

How is the flood test carried out?

The drains are temporarily plugged and a shallow layer of water is held on the roof for 24 to 48 hours while the soffit below is inspected. The slab must be able to carry the load before testing.

Is one layer enough, or do I need two?

For most small residential roofs, one layer of a quality membrane properly installed is enough. On large, low-slope and industrial roofs, and where the membrane will be buried, a two-layer system with staggered laps is recommended.

Conclusion

An Isogam roof membrane stays watertight for years when every stage is done correctly: a dry, clean deck laid to falls, fillets in the corners, fully dried primer, rolls set out from the lowest point with proper laps, even heat, careful detailing, and a flood test at the end. None of these steps is complicated or expensive, but leaving out any one of them puts the whole investment at risk.
