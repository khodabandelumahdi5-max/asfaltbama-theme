Foundation and basement waterproofing is work that can be done properly, at reasonable cost, only once: during construction. After the raft is cast and the basement walls are backfilled, the waterproofing is never seen again, and repairing it is difficult and expensive if possible at all. Meanwhile, the water and salts in the ground work on the structure every day for the life of the building: they corrode reinforcement, attack concrete, make basements damp and mouldy and reduce the value of the property. In the coastal cities of the Gulf and southern Iraq, where groundwater is often shallow and saline and sabkha soils are common, the stakes are even higher.

This guide covers the whole subject: understanding ground conditions, choosing the right system for soil moisture, perched water or pressurised groundwater, the step-by-step installation of membranes under a raft, basement wall waterproofing, critical details such as construction joints and pipe penetrations, drainage and dewatering, and options for existing buildings. The aim is for owners, developers, consultants and site engineers to know exactly how foundation waterproofing should be done and what to check at each stage.

Key points at a glance

  • Foundation waterproofing is installed before concreting on flat, clean, dry blinding.
  • The membrane under the raft must project beyond its edge to be lapped later with the basement wall waterproofing; that junction is the most critical detail.
  • A protection layer is needed over the membrane before reinforcement is fixed.
  • The system must match the groundwater condition: soil moisture is not the same as water under pressure.
  • In wet or saline ground, drainage, dewatering and durable concrete complement the waterproofing; they do not replace it.

Why foundations need waterproofing

The ground around and below a building is almost never completely dry. Soil moisture, rain infiltrating the ground, irrigation of landscaping, leaking water and drainage pipes and, on many sites, groundwater are in constant contact with foundations and basement walls. Concrete is porous and, without protection, absorbs and transmits that water. The consequences:

  • Rising damp: water rises by capillarity through concrete and masonry, causing stains, salt deposits and peeling finishes at ground and basement level.
  • Reinforcement corrosion: moisture, oxygen and chlorides corrode reinforcement; rust expands and cracks the concrete. In the Gulf, chloride-laden groundwater makes this one of the main durability threats to buildings.
  • Sulfate attack: sulfates in soils and groundwater, common in the region's sabkha and gypsiferous soils, react with cement and gradually weaken concrete.
  • Water ingress: where groundwater is high, water under pressure enters through joints and cracks into basements and car parks.
  • Mould and poor air: damp basements grow mould and are unhealthy.
  • Lost value: a damp basement or car park has limited use and is a serious weakness when selling or leasing.

Understand the ground first

  • Groundwater level: did the site investigation boreholes find water, and at what depth? Levels vary seasonally and, near the coast, with tides; in several Gulf cities, rising groundwater has become a recognised problem.
  • Soil type: clays hold water and drain slowly; sands and gravels let it through quickly. Water behaves very differently around foundations in each.
  • Soil and water chemistry: the geotechnical report normally gives sulfate and chloride levels, which govern cement type, concrete quality and the waterproofing specification.
  • Topography and surface water: does the site sit downhill? Does runoff from neighbouring plots flow towards the building? Is there irrigated landscaping against the walls?
  • Neighbouring buildings: damp basements next door are a strong hint about ground conditions.
  • Excavation depth: deep basements are more likely to reach groundwater and face higher water pressure.

Three water conditions and the right system for each

ConditionDescriptionGeneral system
Soil moistureGroundwater well below the foundation; only natural soil moisture and infiltrating rainA single membrane or bitumen and jute under the raft and on the walls, with protection; good surface drainage around the building

Groundwater under pressureGroundwater above the foundation level, permanently or seasonally; typical of many coastal sitesAn engineered system: usually a fully bonded, multi-layer or specialised membrane, low-permeability concrete, waterstops at joints, and dewatering during construction

A common mistake is to install the same "usual" single membrane on every foundation. On a site with high groundwater that is not enough; on a dry site, complex and expensive systems are unnecessary.

Common foundation waterproofing systems

Modified bitumen membranes (Isogam)

Plain (unfaced) APP or SBS membranes torched onto blinding and walls. Uniform factory thickness and quick installation; for foundations, a strong reinforcement is preferable because the membrane is exposed to reinforcement fixing and traffic during construction.

Hot bitumen and jute

Two or more layers, valued for full continuity and adaptability at corners. Quality depends on the crew, and application on vertical walls is more demanding. See our bitumen and jute guide.

Liquid bituminous and polymer coatings

Brushed or sprayed on basement walls; very useful at complex details and around pipes. Dry film thickness must be controlled, and a protection board is usually needed against backfill.

Cementitious and crystalline systems

Applied to concrete surfaces or added to the concrete, they block the pores through a chemical reaction. They can be applied from the inside and tolerate damp surfaces, but have limited capacity to bridge active cracks.

Pre-applied sheet membranes and bentonite

On larger projects with high groundwater, pre-applied HDPE-based membranes that bond to the concrete poured against them, PVC sheets with welded seams or bentonite systems are specified by the designer. They are common on Gulf commercial and infrastructure projects where basements sit in groundwater.

Types of foundation and their effect on waterproofing

Raft foundations

A single slab under the whole building, common with basements. Waterproofing a large, even blinding is simpler, but waiting laps around the whole perimeter and the junction with the walls must be executed carefully. Under groundwater, rafts are usually designed with multi-layer or specialised systems and waterstops.

Strip and pad foundations

The area is split into strips or pads with a ground slab between. Waterproofing must be continuous under and around the footings and under the slab; many corners and level changes make the work more detailed.

Ground-bearing slabs

Basement or car park floors on the ground also face rising damp. A drainage layer, blinding and membrane go beneath, and the floor membrane must connect with the wall and footing waterproofing.

Concrete durability in aggressive ground

Waterproofing is one line of defence; the concrete itself is another. In the chloride- and sulfate-rich ground typical of Gulf coastal sites, the designer normally specifies concrete to resist the exposure: an appropriate cement type or blended cements for sulfate resistance, a low water-cement ratio, adequate cover to the reinforcement and sometimes corrosion-inhibiting admixtures or coated reinforcement. On site, what matters is that this concrete is actually delivered, placed and compacted without honeycombing and cured properly; a well-specified mix that is poorly compacted or allowed to dry out in the heat loses much of its resistance. Good concrete and good waterproofing reinforce each other: the membrane keeps most of the water and salts away, and the concrete resists whatever gets through.

Uplift and flotation

Where groundwater is high, the water pushes upwards on the underside of the basement. A basement that is light, or empty during construction, can be subject to significant uplift, and the waterproofing must be designed with water pressure in mind. This is why dewatering is usually maintained until the structure is heavy enough, and why the decision to stop pumping belongs to the structural engineer. For the waterproofing contractor, the practical point is that systems under hydrostatic pressure must be fully bonded or fully continuous as designed, and every penetration and joint must be treated as a potential leak path under pressure.

Layer sequence under a raft

  1. Compacted formationThe excavation base is trimmed and compacted; loose soil and debris removed.
  2. Drainage layer (where needed)On wet sites, a layer of gravel or granular material.
  3. BlindingLean concrete with a flat, smooth surface: the substrate for the waterproofing.
  4. PrimerOn dry blinding.
  5. WaterproofingMembrane or bitumen and jute, one or more layers depending on conditions.
  6. Protection layerProtective screed or concrete over the membrane.
  7. RaftReinforcement and concrete, with durable concrete specified for the soil chemistry.

Installing a membrane under the raft, step by step

1. Prepare the formation

The excavation base must reach design level, be trimmed and compacted, and be clear of loose soil, debris and stones. If water collects, it must be pumped out and controlled before continuing; blinding and waterproofing cannot be installed under water. On coastal sites, dewatering is often essential throughout foundation works.

2. Flat, sound blinding

The blinding is the substrate for the waterproofing, and its surface quality directly affects the membrane's durability. It must be flat, free of protruding aggregate and sharp edges; any sharp projection under the weight of the raft will puncture the membrane. The surface is trowelled smooth and must be dry before waterproofing. At raft edges and level changes, the blinding is formed so the membrane can pass without sharp folds.

3. Fillets and rounded corners

At level changes, around sumps, lift pits and other recesses, corners are filleted or rounded so the membrane does not crack at sharp angles.

4. Primer

Bitumen primer is applied to dry blinding and allowed to dry fully.

5. Membrane installation

Rolls are torched on with adequate laps, typically 100–150 mm, and laps pressed with a hot trowel. End laps are staggered. Under groundwater, a two-layer system with offset laps, or the designer's specialised system, is usual.

6. Waiting laps for the wall junction

The most critical element. The membrane under the raft must project beyond the raft edge as a "waiting lap" so that the wall waterproofing can later be lapped onto it. This lap is exposed to damage during reinforcement, formwork and concreting and must be protected. If it is torn, contaminated or cut too short, the raft-to-wall junction becomes the main leak path.

7. Protection layer

Before reinforcement is fixed, a protection layer, usually a thin screed or protective concrete, is placed over the membrane. Without it, chairs, bars, tools and foot traffic puncture the membrane in many places, and the damage is then buried under the raft.

8. Inspection before reinforcement

Before the protection layer, the supervisor inspects the whole membrane: laps, corners, penetrations and waiting laps. Any damage is repaired immediately and dated photographs are taken; on supervised projects, this is a formal hold point.

9. Care during reinforcement and concreting

Even with protection, avoid dropping heavy loads, dragging bars and discharging concrete from height onto waiting laps. The waterproofing, reinforcement and concreting teams must coordinate.

10. Pipe penetrations

Every pipe passing through the raft or walls is a potential weak point. Penetrations receive dedicated details such as puddle flanges or sleeves with sealing collars and membrane collars. Penetration positions should be fixed and sleeved before waterproofing so the membrane is not cut afterwards.

Basement wall waterproofing

External (positive side)

The waterproofing is applied to the outer face of the wall, in contact with the soil. This is preferable: water is stopped before reaching the concrete, and water pressure pushes the membrane against the wall. It requires access behind the wall before backfilling.

Internal (negative side)

Where there is no access behind the wall, as in existing buildings or walls against neighbouring plots, the wall is treated from inside, usually with cementitious or crystalline systems. The concrete remains in contact with water and the system must resist pressure from behind.

External waterproofing sequence

  1. Prepare the wallRemove laitance, release agent, tie wire and fins; fill honeycombing and form-tie holes with suitable mortar.
  2. Fillet at the baseAt the wall-to-raft junction.
  3. PrimerOn a dry wall surface.
  4. Connect to the waiting lapThe raft waiting lap is cleaned and prepared and the wall waterproofing lapped onto it.
  5. Wall membraneInstalled from the bottom up with adequate laps, preventing slippage on tall walls.
  6. Top terminationThe waterproofing continues above finished ground level and its top edge is fixed and protected.
  7. Protection and drainage boardBefore backfilling, a protection board or drainage composite is placed over the membrane.
  8. Controlled backfillingIn layers and with care; dumping soil and rocks directly against the wall is not acceptable.

Walls against neighbouring plots and shoring

On urban sites, basement walls are often cast directly against neighbouring plots or shoring, with no access behind them afterwards. Here the waterproofing must be installed before the wall is cast, on the shoring face or a sacrificial block wall, and the concrete wall is then poured against it. This "blind-side" application needs careful planning, a suitable surface to receive the system and a proper connection to the raft waterproofing; pre-applied membranes designed to bond to fresh concrete are widely used for this.

Critical details where most basements leak

  • Raft-to-wall junction: damaged waiting laps or short laps are the most common cause of leaks at the base of basement walls.
  • Construction joints: joints between raft and wall, and between wall pours, are natural water paths. Under groundwater, waterstops (PVC, hydrophilic or injection hoses) are placed in these joints per the design.
  • Form-tie holes: ties passing through the wall must be properly sealed or they become direct water paths.
  • Lift pits and sumps: the lowest points with the highest water pressure; waterproofing must be continuous around and under them.
  • Services penetrations: each needs a sleeve and sealing detail.
  • Movement joints and joints with neighbouring buildings: need dedicated flexible details.

Drainage and dewatering

Waterproofing stops water passing, but water collecting behind a wall still exerts pressure and finds any weakness. Drainage collects and removes it before it reaches the wall:

  • Drainage layer behind the wall: drainage composites or granular fill leading water downwards.
  • Geotextile filter: keeps fine soil out of the drainage layer.
  • Perforated drain at the footing: laid to falls, leading water to a sump or outfall.
  • Sump and pump: where gravity discharge is not possible.
  • Surface drainage: ground sloped away from the building, channels for rainwater and irrigation kept away from walls.

During construction on coastal and low-lying sites, dewatering keeps the excavation dry until the structure is heavy enough and the waterproofing complete; its design and the moment it can be switched off are decided by the engineer, since switching off too early can cause flotation or leaks. Around the finished building, paving and falls also matter: yards and car parks that send water towards the walls load the foundation waterproofing.

Car parks and deep basements

In buildings with two or more basement levels, especially public car parks and large developments, depth raises the likelihood of groundwater and pressure. Waterproofing must be part of the structural design from the start: concrete quality and permeability, construction joint details, the type and number of waterproofing layers, drainage and, where necessary, permanent pumping. In the car park itself, wash-down water and rain entering down ramps must be handled with falls and floor drains. The roof of a car park under a yard or landscaping is itself a "roof" that needs its own waterproofing and protection.

Signs of water entering foundations and basements

  • White salt deposits: salts carried through the wall by water and left behind as it evaporates.
  • Dark damp bands at the base of walls, varying with the season.
  • Peeling plaster and paint in basements and sometimes at ground floor level.
  • Musty smell and mould, even without visible stains.
  • Drips or flowing water from joints, tie holes, penetrations or the floor, especially after prolonged rain or with tidal groundwater.
  • Rust stains or linear cracks that may indicate reinforcement corrosion.

Find the source before acting; not every sign comes from the ground. Leaking pipes and condensation in air-conditioned basements produce similar symptoms.

Existing buildings: what can be done afterwards?

  1. Diagnose the sourceGround water, leaking pipes, rainwater collecting against walls, irrigation or condensation? Removing the source is sometimes simpler than waterproofing.
  2. Reduce water around the buildingRe-grade the ground, direct downpipes away from walls, move irrigated planting and repair leaking pipes.
  3. Internal waterproofingCementitious or crystalline systems on walls and floor from inside, after removing finishes and preparing the surface.
  4. InjectionInjecting active cracks and joints with suitable resins to block water paths.
  5. Internal drainageIn severe cases, cavity drainage membranes, channels and a pumped sump inside the basement.
  6. External excavationWhere access allows, excavating against the wall and installing waterproofing and drainage from outside: the most effective but most expensive option.

Each of these usually costs several times more than doing the waterproofing properly during construction.

Safety when waterproofing in excavations

  • Work in an excavation only once its sides are stable; crews must not work under unsupported faces.
  • Keep gas cylinders outside the excavation or in a ventilated, stable position; LPG is heavier than air and collects at the bottom.
  • Provide ventilation in deep excavations and enclosed spaces for torch fumes and primer vapour.
  • Provide safe access and a safe route for lowering rolls.
  • Where water is present, carry out hot and electrical work only after dewatering and with care.
  • Plan summer work around the heat; excavations can become extremely hot and airless.

Quality control and documentation

  • The blinding is flat, clean and dry with no sharp projections.
  • Primer covers the whole surface and is dry.
  • The waterproofing products match the approved submittal.
  • Laps are adequate and fully fused.
  • Corners and level changes have no sharp folds.
  • Waiting laps are long enough and protected.
  • Penetrations and sleeves have sealing details.
  • Waterstops are installed at construction joints where specified.
  • The protection layer is placed before reinforcement.
  • Wall waterproofing is lapped correctly onto the waiting laps.
  • Protection or drainage boards are in place before backfilling.
  • Dated photographs exist of every stage before it is covered.

Common mistakes

  1. Waterproofing over rough blinding with protruding aggregate.
  2. Working on a wet surface or in an excavation with standing water.
  3. Omitting primer or torching with too little heat.
  4. Cutting waiting laps short or leaving them unprotected.
  5. Fixing reinforcement directly on the membrane without protection.
  6. Cutting the membrane for pipes afterwards without sealing details.
  7. Ignoring construction joints and form-tie holes.
  8. Backfilling with rocks and debris directly against the wall waterproofing.
  9. Using one "standard" system for every site regardless of groundwater and chemistry.
  10. Switching off dewatering too early or omitting drainage on wet sites.

Contract clauses for foundation waterproofing

  • System specification: product types, number of layers, primer and protection, referenced to the approved submittal.
  • Scope: under the raft, walls, lift pits, sumps and ground slabs, each listed separately.
  • Waiting laps: length, responsibility for protecting them until connected, and responsibility for repairing damage caused by others.
  • Protection and drainage: included or not, and specifications.
  • Details: penetrations, construction joints, waterstops and movement joints.
  • Staging: the number of mobilisations matching the construction programme.
  • Inspections and records: hold points before each stage is covered, and dated photographs.
  • Warranty: scope and duration in writing.

Working in hot weather

Foundation waterproofing in the Gulf summer brings particular challenges. Blinding and walls heat up quickly, primer dries fast but dust settles on it just as fast, membranes soften and can be damaged by traffic, and deep excavations become very hot. Crews typically start early, stop in the midday heat, keep rolls shaded, and install the protection layer as soon as the membrane is inspected so that it is not left exposed to sun and traffic longer than necessary. Protection boards on walls should also be placed promptly, since membranes left exposed on walls in full sun can slump or blister before backfilling.

What affects the cost?

  • Ground conditions: soil moisture or groundwater; the system type and number of layers.
  • Area: under the raft and on the basement walls.
  • Details: lift pits, sumps, level changes, the number of penetrations and joints.
  • Access: excavation depth, working space behind walls, blind-side walls.
  • Protection and drainage: protective screed, boards, drains and drainage composites.
  • Coordination: staged work matching reinforcement and concreting.
  • Documentation: submittals, inspections and records on supervised projects.

Foundation waterproofing is usually a small share of the cost of the structure, while curing a damp basement after construction can cost far more.

Questions to settle before choosing a system

  1. What does the geotechnical report say about groundwater level, its seasonal and tidal variation, and sulfate and chloride content?
  2. Will the basement be permanently below groundwater, intermittently, or never?
  3. How will the basement be used: storage, car parking, plant rooms or occupied space? The acceptable level of dampness differs greatly.
  4. Is there access behind all walls, or will some be cast against shoring or neighbouring structures?
  5. How will construction joints, penetrations and lift pits be detailed?
  6. Will dewatering be needed, and who decides when it can stop?
  7. What drainage will be provided, and how will it be maintained?
  8. Which inspections and tests will be carried out before each stage is covered?

Maintaining drainage after handover

Foundation drainage only works if it stays clear. Sumps and pumps need regular checks, particularly before the rainy season; pump failure alarms are worthwhile in basements that rely on pumping. Inspection chambers on perimeter drains should be accessible and flushed if silt builds up. Around the building, keep ground falls and paving directing water away from the walls, and do not allow new irrigated planting against basement walls without considering the waterproofing and drainage beneath.

Frequently asked questions

Can foundations be waterproofed after construction?

Waterproofing under a raft cannot be installed after it is cast. For existing buildings there are options such as reducing water around the building, internal cementitious or crystalline systems, injection and internal drainage, which are usually more expensive.

Is a bitumen membrane or bitumen and jute better for foundations?

Both are used. Membranes give uniform thickness and faster installation; bitumen and jute gives good continuity at corners. Under groundwater, a properly designed continuous system with full details matters more than the product type.

What is different about foundations in the Gulf?

Shallow, saline groundwater and sabkha soils rich in chlorides and sulfates are common near the coast. Durable concrete, a fully continuous waterproofing system, waterstops at joints and dewatering during construction are more often necessary than in inland, dry sites.

How smooth must the blinding be?

Trowelled flat, without protruding aggregate or sharp edges. Any sharp projection can puncture the membrane under the weight of the raft.

Is a protection layer really necessary?

Yes. Without it, reinforcement fixing and traffic damage the membrane in many places, and the damage is buried under the concrete.

How high should basement wall waterproofing go?

At least above finished ground level around the building, with the top edge fixed and protected so surface water cannot get behind it.

Does drainage replace waterproofing?

No. Drainage reduces water pressure and complements the waterproofing but does not replace a continuous waterproof barrier.

Does waterproof concrete replace a membrane?

High-quality, low-permeability concrete with suitable admixtures is part of protection against water, but cracks, construction joints and penetrations remain water paths. In most buildings it complements the waterproofing rather than replacing it, unless the designer has specifically engineered such a system.

Conclusion

Foundation waterproofing offers only one chance to get it right. Understanding the ground, choosing a system suited to soil moisture or groundwater, flat and dry blinding, careful membrane installation, protected waiting laps and a proper junction with the wall waterproofing, a protection layer before reinforcement, sealed penetrations and joints, and suitable drainage and dewatering make the difference between a dry, durable basement and a damp, expensive one.
