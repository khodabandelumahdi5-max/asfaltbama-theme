One of the most common questions we hear on roof surveys is: "Can a new membrane go over the old one, or do we have to strip it?" Re-roofing over an existing membrane is often proposed because it is quicker and cheaper, and in the right conditions it is a perfectly sound choice. But the answer directly affects cost, programme, the load on the slab and, most importantly, the life of the new waterproofing. When the old layer is rotten, wet or debonded, a new membrane laid over it will not last, and today's saving turns into tomorrow's rework, with one more layer to remove.

This guide sets out clear criteria for when laying a new Isogam or modified bitumen membrane over an existing one is acceptable and when stripping is essential, explains simple and specialist ways to assess the old layer, describes the correct procedure for both routes, overlaying and full tear-off, and covers practical points such as drains, roof load, cost and warranty. It is written for owners, facility managers, consultants and main contractors dealing with ageing roofs in Iraq and the Gulf.

Key points at a glance

  • A new membrane may go over an old one only when the old layer is sound, dry, flat and fully bonded.
  • If the old layer is blistered, rotten, debonded or wet, or there is more than one old layer, strip it.
  • Assess the old layer with adhesion and moisture tests, not by eye.
  • A new membrane does not bond to the foil of an old one; the foil must be removed or the surface prepared.
  • Every extra layer raises the roof level and adds load, which affects drains, thresholds and upstands.

Why the decision matters

At first glance, overlaying is simply the cheaper option: no stripping, no disposal and less substrate repair. But it has three important consequences:

  • Bond: the new membrane is only as well attached as the layer beneath it. If the old layer is no longer bonded to the slab, neither, in effect, is the new one; any movement or lifting of the old layer takes the new one with it.
  • Trapped moisture: if there is water under or within the old layer, the new membrane seals it in. As the roof heats up in the sun, the moisture turns to vapour and raises large blisters under the new membrane, a process that is especially fast under Gulf summer temperatures.
  • Weight and level: each layer adds dead load and raises the roof surface, which affects drain outlets, roof door thresholds and the effective height of upstands.

That is why the decision must be based on a technical assessment, not only on price.

Typical conditions of an existing roof

A sound membrane with surface wear

The membrane is fully bonded, but the foil has worn in places, the bitumen shows fine surface crazing and a few laps or edges need repair. This is the best case for overlaying, provided preparation is right.

A membrane with scattered blisters

Most of the surface is bonded but there are some blisters. If they are few and dry, they can be cut, dried and repaired before overlaying. If they are many, large and full of water, it indicates widespread debonding and moisture, and stripping makes more sense.

A rotten, debonded membrane

Reinforcement fibres show through, the bitumen is brittle, the membrane tears by hand and large areas have lifted. A new membrane cannot go over this; stripping is essential.

Old bitumen and jute

Sound, bonded and dry bitumen and jute can be overlaid after cleaning, repairs and priming. Rotten, deeply cracked material, or bitumen that has slumped down the parapets, must be removed.

Several layers stacked up

On older buildings it is common to find an old bitumen system, a membrane on top and another membrane above that. Adding a fourth layer is almost never advisable: weight has built up, moisture is trapped between layers and the bond of the whole stack is unknown. Stripping everything and starting on a sound substrate is the most economical long-term route.

Tiled roofs over old waterproofing

If a tiled roof is leaking through the waterproofing beneath, torching a membrane directly onto the tiles is not usually recommended: tile joints, unevenness and moisture below reduce adhesion and the falls are not corrected. The condition beneath the tiles must be assessed and the tiles usually lifted.

Inverted roofs

On inverted roofs, the old membrane lies under insulation boards and ballast. Before deciding, the ballast and boards are lifted in several areas to inspect the membrane. Wet or damaged insulation boards are usually replaced during re-roofing.

Eight checks on the old layer

  1. Visual survey of the whole surfaceCracks, blisters, wrinkles, open laps, exposed fibres, the state of the foil, upstands and the details at drains and pipes. Sketch a simple defect map.
  2. Tap and press testWalk the roof and tap with a tool handle. Areas that sound hollow or feel soft underfoot have debonded. If more than a small part of the roof is like this, the overall bond is doubtful.
  3. Adhesion testAt several locations, cut a small triangular or cross-shaped flap and try to lift it. A well-bonded membrane resists and leaves bitumen stuck to the substrate; if the flap lifts easily and the surface beneath is dusty or clean and dry, the bond is poor.
  4. Moisture testCheck whether it is wet beneath the membrane at the test cuts. On larger roofs, a moisture meter or small cores reveal whether the screed to falls holds water. A wet screed will not dry out under a new membrane.
  5. Count the layersA cut or core shows how many layers are present. More than one old layer usually means stripping.
  6. Falls and pondingOverlaying does not correct falls. Where there are hollows, the waterproofing in those areas usually has to be removed to fix them.
  7. Details and levelsCheck remaining parapet height above the new surface, roof door threshold height and drain outlet levels. An extra layer changes all of them.
  8. Roof loadOn older buildings, lightweight roofs and roofs already re-covered several times, consider the extra weight and consult a structural engineer if in doubt.

Specialist methods on larger roofs

  • Moisture meters: readings on a regular grid map wet areas under the membrane so that cuts are made only where needed.
  • Infrared thermography: after sunset, wet areas under the membrane retain heat longer and show up differently on a thermal camera, which helps find large areas of trapped moisture. In the Gulf, the strong daytime heating makes this method particularly effective.
  • Core sampling: a small core through all layers down to the slab shows the number, thickness and moisture of each layer; the core hole is then carefully repaired.
  • Test patch: a small piece of new membrane torched onto a prepared area of the old one, left to cool and then tested for adhesion before deciding for the whole roof.

On a large roof, the cost of these checks is trivial compared with the cost of the wrong decision.

What a good survey report contains

  • Number and type of existing layers.
  • Adhesion test results at identified locations.
  • Presence or absence of moisture under the layers and in the screed.
  • Number and approximate location of blisters, open laps and debonded areas.
  • Condition of details: upstands, drains, pipes and plinths.
  • Hollows and ponding areas.
  • Conclusion: overlay, full strip or a combination, with reasons.
  • Dated photographs of the tested points.

The report is the basis of the quotation and, if problems appear later, a clear reference for the warranty.

Comparing the two routes

Overlay

  • Lower cost: no stripping, disposal or full substrate repair.
  • Faster, cleaner site.
  • The roof is rarely left exposed to rain during the work.
  • A sound old layer can act as an additional layer.
  • But: only for sound, dry old layers; adds weight and height; falls are not corrected.

Strip and replace

  • The substrate can be seen and repaired, falls corrected and fillets formed.
  • Moisture and doubtful layers are removed; the new system starts on a sound base.
  • Load is reduced on multi-layer roofs.
  • Clearer warranty, since the whole system is new.
  • But: more cost and time; waste must be managed and the roof protected from rain during the work.

An illustrative comparison: a 150 m² roof in two conditions

This is a teaching example, without prices.

Case A: one sound old membrane

Tests show the old membrane is bonded and dry over most of the roof; only the foil has worn and there are problems around two drains and one AC plinth. Logical route: clean, remove the foil, repair a few blisters, rebuild the details at the drains and plinth, overlay and test. Quick, little waste, and the roof is hardly ever left exposed.

Case B: two old layers and ponding

Under the top membrane there is an old bitumen system, water appears in several test cuts and one corner has a large hollow. Logical route: strip both layers in sections, let the substrate dry, correct the falls in the hollow, form fillets, prime, install the new membrane and test. More time and cost, but a sound system on a sound base, and the weight of the old layers comes off the slab.

In case B, laying a third membrane over two wet layers would very likely lead to blisters and leaks within the first years, and eventually three layers would have to be stripped.

Procedure for overlaying an existing membrane

  1. Thorough cleaningRemove sand, silt, algae, gravel and anything sharp; sweep and blow the surface. Dust on the old membrane destroys the bond of the new one. After a dust storm, clean again.
  2. Repair blistersCut each blister in a cross, dry and clean beneath, re-bond the flaps with heat and patch where needed.
  3. Cut wrinkles and ridgesLong wrinkles and raised old laps are cut and flattened so the surface under the new membrane is even.
  4. Remove or prepare the foilThe most important technical point: a new membrane does not bond to aluminium foil. The foil must be burned and scraped off or the surface prepared as the manufacturer specifies, so the new membrane fuses with the old bitumen. A membrane torched onto foil is merely lying on the roof, not bonded to it.
  5. Repair old laps and edgesOpen laps and lifted edges of the old layer are re-bonded.
  6. Prime where neededWhere concrete or screed is exposed, and on the old surface if the manufacturer recommends it, prime and let dry.
  7. Rebuild the detailsUpstands, drains, pipes and plinths are usually the weakest part of the old layer and are best opened back to a sound substrate and rebuilt.
  8. Install the new layerFrom the lowest point, with at least 100 mm laps, offset from the old laps, fully torched.
  9. New upstandsThe new membrane must go at least 200–300 mm above the new finished surface and be mechanically fixed. Because the roof level has risen, the old upstand height may no longer be enough.
  10. Flood testA 24–48 hour test with the result recorded.

Procedure for stripping the old waterproofing

  1. Plan in sectionsDivide the roof into areas that can be stripped and re-waterproofed within a day or two. A roof should not be left without waterproofing for days, particularly when rain is forecast.
  2. Cut and liftThe old waterproofing is cut into manageable strips with scrapers, cutters or powered tools, taking care not to damage a sound substrate unnecessarily.
  3. Remove and disposeWaste is bagged or bundled, lowered safely and taken to a licensed disposal site. Throwing waste off the roof is dangerous.
  4. Clean the substrateResidual bitumen, loose mortar and dust are removed.
  5. Inspect and repairCracks, voids, loose screed and moisture in lightweight screeds are now visible and repaired; a wet screed must be given time to dry or be replaced.
  6. Correct falls and form filletsThe best opportunity to fix hollows and form fillets in corners.
  7. Install the new waterproofingWith primer, laps, full details and a flood test.

Safety during stripping

Old waterproofing is often heavier than expected and carrying it across roofs and stairs is hazardous. Never throw waste from the roof, protect open edges, use powered tools carefully away from pipes and cables, and plan work around the midday heat in summer.

Partial stripping: joining new to old

  1. A clean edgeThe old waterproofing is cut in a straight, clean line at the edge of the area, not torn off irregularly.
  2. Level the areaThe stripped area is repaired and, if needed, brought level with mortar so there is no sharp step at the edge.
  3. Prepare the old edgeFoil and dirt are removed from a band at least 150 mm wide around the area.
  4. Adequate lapThe new membrane laps at least 150 mm onto the prepared old layer and is fully fused.
  5. Lap directionThe top edge of the lap faces down the slope.

Drains when a layer is added

When a new membrane goes over an old one, the roof surface rises slightly but the drain outlet stays at its original level, creating a small step where water collects before entering the drain. To prevent this:

  • The old waterproofing is removed around the outlet so the area around the drain stays low.
  • The new membrane is dressed into the outlet with a reinforcing piece.
  • Where needed, retrofit drain inserts or outlets designed for re-roofing are used.
  • After installation, a water test confirms that water flows into the drain without ponding.

Screeds, insulation and trapped water

Many roofs have a lightweight screed or insulation beneath the waterproofing. If the old waterproofing has leaked for a long time, that layer has absorbed water: its insulating value has dropped and it becomes a source of vapour for future blisters. Overlaying seals that water in for years. Stripping and allowing the screed to dry, or replacing wet sections, is the correct solution; wet insulation boards are normally replaced. On inverted roofs, wet or damaged boards are replaced during re-roofing.

Roof load and number of layers

Each membrane layer weighs several kilograms per square metre, depending on thickness and type. A single layer is rarely an issue. But on older buildings where old bitumen, several membranes, extra screeds and sometimes tiles have accumulated, the total can be significant, especially when wet. Our practical rule is that no more than one old waterproofing layer should remain under a new membrane. If the slab is old and shows cracking, deflection or corroded reinforcement, have the structure assessed before adding anything.

Cost: which route is really cheaper?

Overlaying avoids stripping, disposal and some substrate repair, and is cheaper in the short term, but only if the old layer is genuinely sound. If it is not:

  • The new membrane blisters, lifts or leaks sooner.
  • Repeated repairs cost money, and leak damage to the slab and interior adds up.
  • Eventually both layers must be stripped, so the stripping cost saved today moves to the future, with an extra layer.

The right question is not "which is cheaper?" but "given the condition of my roof, which route costs less over the life of the waterproofing?"

Documenting the decision on supervised projects

On projects supervised by a consultant or managed by a facility management company, the decision to overlay or strip should be recorded formally. A short technical note, supported by the survey report, test results and photographs, should state the condition of the existing roof, the chosen approach and the reasons, and be approved before work starts. The method statement then describes the preparation steps specific to the chosen route: foil removal and blister repair for an overlay, or phasing, waste disposal and substrate drying for a tear-off. Inspection hold points are set accordingly, for example after cleaning and foil removal, after detail rebuilding and before the flood test. This documentation protects the owner, the consultant and the contractor if questions arise later.

Budgeting for hidden defects

Old roofs hide surprises. Once the old waterproofing is lifted, it is common to find wet screed, cracked or spalled concrete, corroded reinforcement near drains, blocked or broken outlets, or old openings sealed with nothing but bitumen. A realistic budget includes a contingency for such findings and a clear procedure for pricing them, for example agreed unit rates for screed replacement or concrete repair. On overlays, hidden defects are less visible but not absent, which is another reason to base the decision on test cuts rather than a surface inspection alone.

Signs that an overlay was not done properly

  • New blisters, particularly growing on sunny days after cooler weather: moisture trapped between layers.
  • Lifting roll edges or laps that open with a spatula: too little heat or bonding onto foil.
  • Long ripples and wrinkles: movement of the old layer beneath.
  • Ponding around drains: a step created at the outlet.
  • Leaks along walls: upstands too short after the roof level rose.

If you see these signs, notify the contractor within the warranty period and before the rainy season.

Occupied buildings and rooftop equipment

Stripping old waterproofing on an occupied building is noisy and produces a lot of waste. Agree working hours with residents or tenants, protect the route used to remove waste, coordinate the moving of AC units and antennas with their owners, and programme the work so the roof is never left open overnight or when rain is forecast. Overlaying avoids most of this disruption, which is one reason it is attractive when conditions allow.

Where there is equipment that is hard to move, such as solar arrays, large water tanks or central plant, the work is usually phased so the equipment is moved in stages, the waterproofing beneath is inspected and replaced if needed, and the equipment returned on proper plinths or supports. Waterproofing only the spaces between equipment and leaving old waterproofing beneath it creates weak points that leak later.

Re-roofing in the Gulf climate

Heat changes the calculation in several ways. Trapped moisture turns to vapour much faster under a roof that reaches extreme temperatures, so blisters from a wet old layer appear sooner and grow larger than in temperate climates. Daily expansion and contraction is greater, so a new membrane bonded to a weak old layer is stressed every day. And summer working conditions limit the hours available for stripping, which pushes contractors towards overlaying for speed. The practical consequences: be stricter about moisture in the old layer, prefer stripping where there is doubt, schedule tear-off work for the cooler months where possible, and when working in summer, plan smaller sections that can be stripped and re-covered within the cooler hours of a single day.

Questions to ask the contractor

  1. How did you assess the old layer? Where did you test adhesion and moisture, and what were the results?
  2. How many layers are on the roof now, and how many will remain?
  3. How will you deal with the foil on the old membrane?
  4. Which details will you open back to the substrate and rebuild?
  5. How will you handle the drains and the change of level?
  6. How high will the new upstands be above the new surface, and how will they be fixed?
  7. If you strip, how will you protect the roof from rain and dispose of the waste?
  8. Will you carry out a flood test, and what exactly does your warranty cover?

Maintenance after an overlay

An overlaid roof needs the same care as any other, with a few particular points. In the first year, inspect the roof after the first rains and on hot sunny days so any blisters are caught early. Keep drains clean and watch the junction between the new membrane and the outlet. And check upstands at every inspection, because they have become more critical after the roof level rose. Clearing sand after dust storms and keeping AC condensate piped to drains remain as important as ever.

Clarify the warranty

  • What does it cover: the new layer, the rebuilt details, leaks?
  • How are problems originating in the old layer, such as blisters from trapped moisture, handled?
  • What checks did the contractor make before the work, and are the results written down?

A contractor who proposes overlaying without testing adhesion and moisture, yet offers a full warranty, should explain what that warranty is based on.

Common mistakes

  1. Deciding by looking at the surface, without adhesion and moisture tests.
  2. Torching a new membrane onto old foil without removing or preparing it.
  3. Leaving old blisters under the new layer.
  4. Adding a third or fourth layer to multi-layer roofs.
  5. Ignoring the change in level at drains and door thresholds.
  6. Relying on old upstands that are too short once the roof level rises.
  7. Overlaying a roof with hollows and ponding.
  8. Stripping the whole roof at once in the rainy season.
  9. Damaging a sound substrate while stripping.
  10. Skipping the final flood test.

Other systems over existing waterproofing

Sometimes a reinforced liquid system is proposed instead of a membrane as an overlay on a sound old roof. It is lighter, needs no flame and works well at complex details, but the same basic condition applies: the old layer must be sound, dry and bonded, and the product must be compatible with bitumen. On roofs with sprayed foam, the old foam is assessed in the same way; degraded foam is cut out and replaced before a new coating or system is applied.

Frequently asked questions

Can a membrane go over old bitumen and jute?

If it is sound, dry and fully bonded, yes, after cleaning, removing high spots, repairs and priming where needed. Rotten, deeply cracked or blistered bitumen and jute must be removed.

How many layers are acceptable on a roof?

As a practical rule, no more than one old waterproofing layer should remain under a new membrane. Roofs with two or more old layers should normally be stripped to the substrate.

Will a new membrane definitely stop an existing leak?

Not necessarily. If the leak is caused by poor falls, drains, parapets or render, a new membrane will not solve it. Find the cause first; see our guide to roof leaks after waterproofing.

Does the old foil have to be removed?

Yes, or the surface must be prepared as the manufacturer specifies. A new membrane does not bond to aluminium foil; it must fuse with the old bitumen.

How do I know if there is water under the old membrane?

Through small test cuts at several points, especially at blisters and hollows, or with a moisture meter, thermography or cores. Areas that sound hollow or are blistered are the most suspect.

Does overlaying shorten the life of the new membrane?

If the old layer is sound and dry and preparation is correct, the new membrane's life is usually similar to installation on a bare substrate, and the old layer can act as an extra layer. If the old layer is defective, the new membrane's life is greatly reduced.

The contractor says stripping is not necessary. Should I trust that?

Ask for the adhesion and moisture test results in writing, and for the warranty to state explicitly that the contractor accepts responsibility for the new layer on the existing surface. A proposal made without testing is only a guess.

How long must the substrate dry after stripping?

It depends on moisture content, substrate type and weather. A screed topping may dry in a day or two in hot, dry weather, but lightweight screed that has been wet for a long time may need weeks. A polythene sheet test is a simple indicator.

Conclusion

Laying a new membrane over an old one is neither always forbidden nor always acceptable. When there is a single old layer that is sound, dry and fully bonded, overlaying with proper preparation, foil removal, blister repair, rebuilt details and a flood test is a sensible and economical choice. When the old layer is rotten, blistered, wet or multi-layered, or the roof has hollows, stripping and starting on a sound substrate is the only lasting solution. Decide on the basis of adhesion and moisture tests, not appearance or price alone.
