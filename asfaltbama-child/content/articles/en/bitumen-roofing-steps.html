Hot bitumen and jute roofing, known in Iran as qirguni and in Iraq and the Gulf as waterproofing with bitumen and hessian (القير والجنفاص), is the oldest bituminous waterproofing method still in everyday use. Even though prefabricated membranes such as Isogam have become common, hot bitumen and jute roofing remains the best choice in many situations: roofs that will be finished with tiles or a screed, foundations and basement walls, wet areas, and surfaces with complicated shapes or many details. Unlike a factory-made membrane, a bitumen and jute system is built entirely in place, so its final quality depends almost completely on the crew: the temperature of the bitumen, the number of layers, the laps of the jute and the care taken at the edges.

This guide explains the complete process of hot bitumen and jute roofing, from choosing the bitumen and fabric and estimating quantities, through deck preparation and the layer-by-layer build-up, to parapet and drain details, flood testing and the protective layer. It closes with the common defects of old bitumen roofs, how to repair them, and a quality checklist you can use to assess a contractor's work yourself.

Key points at a glance

  • A standard roof system has two layers of jute: bitumen, jute, bitumen, jute, bitumen.
  • The deck must be dry, clean, laid to falls and filleted, and it receives a primer before the first hot coat.
  • Jute strips are lapped by about 100 mm, and the laps of the second layer never sit over those of the first.
  • The bitumen must be hot and fluid, but never so hot that it smokes and burns.
  • Exposed bitumen and jute cracks quickly in the sun and needs a protective finish.

What is bitumen and jute roofing, and where is it still the best choice?

Bitumen and jute roofing is a built-up, multi-layer waterproofing made from hot bitumen and jute (hessian) fabric. The bitumen is the waterproof element; the jute reinforces it, much as steel reinforces concrete. The fabric stops the bitumen from cracking, stretching and flowing, and gives the system an even thickness. The result is a continuous, seamless skin that is fully bonded to the surface beneath.

That continuity and full bond is the method's greatest strength. Because the hot bitumen is poured and spread on the surface, it flows into every irregularity, corner and detail and leaves no voids behind the waterproofing. That is why many engineers still specify it for:

  • Roofs that will receive tiles, screed or another finish: the buried waterproofing is protected from the sun, which removes the main weakness of bitumen and jute.
  • Foundations, rafts and basement walls: where the waterproofing is in permanent contact with damp soil and must bond fully to the concrete. In the Gulf, where saline groundwater and sabkha soils attack concrete, a continuous bituminous barrier is especially valuable.
  • Bathrooms, kitchens and other wet areas: under the floor finish and partly up the walls.
  • Irregular and busy surfaces: roofs with many pipes, plinths, ducts and corners where cutting and fitting membrane rolls is difficult.
  • Local reinforcement: around drains, at roof-to-wall junctions and at details that are sometimes reinforced with bitumen and jute even in a membrane system.

Advantages and limitations

Advantages

  • A continuous, seamless waterproofing fully bonded to the substrate.
  • Excellent adaptation to irregular surfaces, corners and complex details.
  • Extra layers can be added at critical points.
  • Relatively simple repairs with the same materials.
  • Good performance under protective finishes such as tiles.

Limitations

  • Quality depends entirely on workmanship and is less uniform than a factory product.
  • Sensitive to direct sun; unprotected, it cracks early.
  • Hot bitumen brings burn and fire risks and a messier site.
  • Installation takes longer than a membrane.
  • Ordinary bitumen softens in extreme heat and becomes brittle in severe cold.

For exposed roofs, a modified bitumen membrane is often the faster option; our Isogam roof membrane installation guide explains that system step by step.

Materials: bitumen, jute and primer

Choosing the bitumen

Not every bitumen is suitable for waterproofing. The bitumen for a roof must not flow down the slope in summer heat and must not become brittle in winter. Two families are common:

  • Oxidised (blown) bitumen: grades such as 85/25 and 90/15. Air is blown through these bitumens at the refinery, which stiffens them and raises their softening point, so they resist heat better. For roofs and sloping surfaces exposed to the sun, oxidised bitumen is usually specified. In the Iraqi and Gulf summer, when roof surfaces become extremely hot, a high softening point is essential.
  • Penetration-grade bitumen 60/70: the paving grade, used on some projects under floor finishes and in foundations away from the sun, according to the specification. On an exposed roof it softens more than oxidised bitumen.

The numbers in the name matter: for oxidised grades the first number roughly indicates the softening point and the second the penetration. The higher the softening point, the more heat-resistant the bitumen. The bitumen grade should always be written into the contract; price differences between grades are significant, and quietly substituting a cheaper grade is a common way to cut quality.

Jute fabric

Jute (hessian) is a coarse fabric woven from plant fibres. Fabric for waterproofing should be:

  • New and sound: free of tears, rot and holes. Second-hand sacking has weak, contaminated fibres.
  • Dry: damp jute does not absorb bitumen and releases steam under the hot coat, causing blisters. Store it under cover and off the ground; in humid coastal cities this matters even more.
  • Evenly woven: not so dense that bitumen cannot pass through it, nor so open that it adds no strength.
  • Clean: free of soil, oil and anything else that reduces adhesion.

Some specifications replace jute with a polyester or glass-fibre scrim, which does not rot. The reinforcement type should follow the project specification.

Primer

Primer is bitumen thinned with solvent and applied to the substrate before the first hot coat. It penetrates the pores of the screed, binds dust and creates a bituminous surface to which the hot bitumen fuses. Hot bitumen poured onto a dry, dusty screed sits largely on the dust rather than on the surface.

Estimating quantities

As with a membrane, the waterproofing area is the plan area of the roof plus the upstands on parapets, stair enclosures and plinths. Then:

  • Jute: for a two-layer system, the fabric area is roughly twice the waterproofing area, plus about 10 to 15 percent for laps and cutting.
  • Bitumen: consumption depends on the roughness and absorbency of the surface, the thickness of each coat and the number of coats. For a two-layer system it is usually several kilograms per square metre, often estimated at around 5 to 7 kg/m²; the contractor should confirm the figure for the actual surface and specification.
  • Primer: according to the manufacturer and the absorbency of the substrate.

A practical tip: instead of the vague phrase "two-layer bitumen and jute", a good contract states the bitumen grade, the fabric type, the number of layers, the height of upstands and the type of protective finish. Two contractors can both write "two layers" and still use very different amounts of bitumen.

Equipment and site set-up

Because the system is built with hot bitumen, site set-up is an important part of the job:

  • Bitumen boiler: on a stable, non-combustible base, away from combustible materials, ducts and windows, and positioned so that any spill cannot run onto people or facades.
  • Lifting hot bitumen to the roof: in sound steel buckets, by rope or hoist, along a clear, controlled route.
  • Spreading tools: bitumen brooms, brushes or mops, and trowels for pressing the jute.
  • Thermometer: professional crews check the bitumen temperature with a thermometer, not by eye.
  • Safety equipment: heat-resistant gloves, safety boots, long sleeves, eye protection and a fire extinguisher.

Step-by-step: two-layer bitumen and jute roofing

Step 1: Survey and decide about the old waterproofing

If the roof has been waterproofed before, decide whether the old layers will be removed or the new system laid over them. Old bitumen and jute that is rotten, cracked, blistered and delaminated is not a suitable base and must be stripped. If the existing waterproofing is sound and fully bonded, it can sometimes be cleaned, repaired and overlaid. Remember that every extra layer adds dead load to the slab.

Step 2: Clean and repair the substrate

Remove soil, gravel, debris, old bitumen and anything sharp. Grind off mortar ridges and fill cracks and holes with mortar. The substrate must be completely dry: moisture under hot bitumen turns to steam and forms blisters that later crack. After dust storms, sweep again before priming.

Step 3: Lay the roof to falls

No waterproofing replaces correct falls. A fall of about 1.5 to 2 percent towards the drains is formed with lightweight concrete or screed and checked with a level and string lines. A simple water test after screeding shows whether any hollows remain. Ponding water on bitumen and jute, especially combined with silt, gradually degrades the bitumen and rots the fabric.

Step 4: Form fillets at internal corners

At the junction between the roof and parapets, around stair enclosures and plinths, a triangular or rounded mortar fillet turns the 90-degree angle into a sloping face. Fabric cannot bond fully into a sharp corner; the void left behind it later becomes a crack and a leak. Fillets must be fully cured before priming.

Step 5: Apply the primer

Bitumen primer is brushed or rolled evenly over the whole surface and the parts of walls that the waterproofing will run up, and it must dry completely. Pouring hot bitumen onto primer that still contains solvent is a fire risk and traps solvent under the system.

Step 6: Heat the bitumen and control its temperature

The bitumen is heated gradually and evenly in the boiler until fully fluid. The working temperature should stay within the range stated by the bitumen producer: hot enough to spread easily and soak into the jute, but not so hot that it smokes heavily, foams or burns.

Bitumen too cool

  • Thick and uneven coats.
  • Does not soak into the jute, leaving dry fibres.
  • Does not fuse with the coat below, so layers separate.

Bitumen overheated

  • Burns, loses adhesion and flexibility and becomes brittle.
  • Produces heavy smoke and fumes and raises the fire risk.
  • Can scorch the jute.

Repeated or prolonged heating also degrades bitumen. A batch that has been kept boiling for hours, cooled and reheated is no longer the material it was on the first morning.

Step 7: First hot coat

Hot bitumen is poured over an area small enough that the jute can be laid before it cools, and spread evenly with a broom or mop. Work starts at the lowest point of the roof, beside the drains. Coat thickness must be even; bare or thin spots become paths for water through the fabric.

Step 8: Lay the first layer of jute

The jute is laid immediately onto the hot bitumen and pressed with a trowel or broom until it is fully embedded and bitumen rises through the weave. Key points:

  • Strips start at the bottom of the slope, and each strip overlaps the one below so water runs over the laps, not into them.
  • Side and end laps are about 100 mm.
  • The fabric must lie flat and without wrinkles; wrinkles form small channels for water.
  • Do not stretch the fabric; stretched jute pulls away from corners when the bitumen cools.
  • No part of the jute may remain dry; dry fabric wicks water through the system.

Step 9: Second hot coat

Once the first layer of jute is fully embedded, a second coat of hot bitumen is applied over it, covering the whole surface and penetrating the weave so the two bitumen coats fuse through the fabric.

Step 10: Second layer of jute

The second layer of jute is laid at right angles to the first, or parallel to it but offset by about half a strip width, so that no lap in the second layer sits over a lap in the first. The same rules apply: pressed flat, no wrinkles, fully saturated.

Step 11: Final coat

A final coat of hot bitumen covers the second layer of jute so that no fibres remain exposed. Exposed fibres let moisture into the system and rot quickly in the sun. The finished surface should be uniform, glossy and free of dry spots.

Step 12: Upstands, edges and details

As with membranes, most leaks in bitumen and jute systems start at details:

  • Parapets: the system is taken at least 200–300 mm up the wall, and its top edge is tucked into a chase or protected with mortar, a metal flashing or a suitable capping so water cannot get behind it.
  • Drains: bitumen and jute are dressed into the drain outlet with an extra reinforcing layer around it. The outlet must be the lowest point of the roof.
  • Pipes and plinths: every penetration receives extra bitumen and jute that runs up the pipe and is secured with a clamp or band.
  • Expansion joints: need a dedicated flexible detail and must not be treated like the rest of the roof.

At critical points, a third layer of bitumen and jute as a reinforcing strip costs little and adds a great deal of security.

Step 13: Flood test

Before the protective layer or tiles go down, the drains are plugged and a shallow depth of water is held on the roof for 24 to 48 hours while the soffit below is inspected. Confirm that the slab can carry the load, and keep the water below the height of the upstands. This test is critical when the roof will be tiled, because once tiles are laid, finding a leak is extremely difficult.

Step 14: Protective layer

Sun and heat gradually oxidise bitumen and make it brittle, so an exposed bitumen and jute roof cracks early. It should never be left unprotected. Common options:

  • Tiles or a screed: a separation layer, then mortar and tiles. The best protection against sun, impact and foot traffic, and the most common finish on flat roofs across Iraq and the Gulf.
  • Asphalt on the roof: used on some buildings and large roofs, giving good protection.
  • Aluminium or reflective coating: reflects sunlight and lowers surface temperature; it must be designed for bitumen and renewed every few years.
  • Sand blinding: clean sand scattered onto the final coat while it is still hot; simple and inexpensive, but limited protection.

Foundations and basement walls

Below ground, the waterproofing is permanently in contact with damp soil and sometimes groundwater, and once backfilled it is never seen again, so precision matters even more than on a roof:

  • Substrate: under a raft, the system is laid on blinding concrete, which must be flat, clean and dry. Stones and sharp edges puncture the waterproofing under the weight of the foundation.
  • Laps to the walls: the waterproofing under the raft must project beyond its edge so it can later be joined to the basement wall waterproofing. This junction is one of the most critical details and needs adequate laps and protection of the waiting edge.
  • Protection before concreting: a protective screed is laid over the waterproofing so reinforcement, chairs and workers do not damage it.
  • Basement walls: the system goes on the outer face of the wall, the side in contact with soil, and is covered with a protection board or layer before backfilling so stones cannot damage it.

In saline ground, the waterproofing specification should be decided with the designer; bitumen and jute, membranes or combinations of the two are all used.

Bathrooms, kitchens and wet areas

In wet areas the system goes under the floor finish and turns up the walls, higher within the shower zone. The floor must be laid to falls towards the floor drain before waterproofing, and pipes and drains must be sealed with particular care. After waterproofing and before tiling, a flood test of the floor is essential, because repairing a bathroom leak means breaking out tiles and screed.

Layer sequence on a tiled roof

One of the most common uses of bitumen and jute is under tiled roof finishes. The typical sequence from the bottom up is:

  1. Structural slabConcrete slab or rib-and-block slab, cleaned and repaired.
  2. FallsLightweight concrete or screed laid to falls towards the drains, with a smooth topping.
  3. PrimerBitumen primer on a dry surface.
  4. Two-layer bitumen and juteWith full upstands and details, followed by a flood test.
  5. Separation layerA polythene sheet or protection layer that separates the mortar from the waterproofing and prevents mechanical damage.
  6. Thermal insulation where specifiedIn the Gulf, roofs often carry thermal insulation boards; their position relative to the waterproofing is set by the design.
  7. Mortar and tilesWith movement joints in the tiling so that thermal movement of the tiles does not stress the waterproofing below.

Omitting the separation layer, or tiling without movement joints, is a frequent cause of damage to the waterproofing under tiled roofs, especially in climates with large daily temperature swings.

Combining bitumen and jute with membranes

Some projects combine the two methods to get the advantages of both:

  • Bitumen and jute at details, membrane on the field: drains, corners and pipes are reinforced with bitumen and jute and the main roof area is covered with a membrane, combining the flexibility of the built-up system at details with the speed and uniformity of the membrane.
  • Bitumen and jute below, membrane on top: on sensitive or large roofs, a bitumen and jute base layer is covered with a foil-faced membrane cap sheet. The base must be fully cool and clean before the membrane is torched on.

Quality control during the work

Once finished, a bitumen and jute system hides most of its quality, so control has to happen during the work, not only at handover. Check that:

  • The substrate is dry, clean, laid to falls and filleted before priming.
  • The primer is fully dry before hot bitumen is applied.
  • The bitumen grade and fabric match the contract; check the packaging.
  • The bitumen spreads fluidly and evenly without heavy smoke or a burnt smell.
  • The jute lies flat, fully saturated, with laps of about 100 mm.
  • The second layer of jute is at right angles to, or offset from, the first.
  • No fibres are exposed in the final coat.
  • Upstands are at least 200–300 mm and their top edge is protected.
  • Drains and pipes have reinforcing layers.
  • A flood test has been carried out before the protective finish.

A simple habit for owners and supervisors: take dated photographs of each stage, especially of the first layer of jute before the second coat, and of parapet and drain details. They are valuable both for warranty claims and for diagnosing any future problem.

Diagnosing and repairing old bitumen roofs

Many older buildings still have bitumen and jute roofs. If yours is several years old, learn to recognise these signs:

Surface crazing

Fine, networked cracks on an exposed bitumen roof show that the bitumen has aged in the sun. If the cracks are superficial and the layers are still bonded, cleaning, a fresh coat of bitumen and a protective finish can extend its life. If the cracks are deep and the jute is visible, patching is only temporary.

Blisters

Blisters usually come from moisture trapped under the waterproofing. Small, stable blisters can be cut, dried and patched with bitumen and jute. Many large blisters indicate widespread loss of bond.

Bitumen flowing in summer

If bitumen slides down walls or slopes in the heat, the wrong grade was probably used or there is no protective layer. Upstands and edges gradually lose their waterproofing.

Rotten jute

When jute fibres stick out of the surface and crumble in the hand, the system has reached the end of its life. Stripping and replacing it is usually more economical than repeated patching.

Patching a bitumen and jute roof

When the system is sound overall and damaged only in a defined area, such as around a drain, where a plinth has been removed or at a local crack, a local repair makes sense:

  1. Mark the areaThe damaged area plus at least 150–200 mm all round is marked out.
  2. Remove loose materialRotten bitumen and jute, blisters and delaminated parts are cut away back to sound, bonded material.
  3. Clean and drySoil, silt and moisture are removed. A patch on a wet surface does not last.
  4. PrimePrimer goes on the exposed substrate and the edges of the surrounding sound system.
  5. Build up the layersTwo layers of bitumen and jute are applied, each extending beyond the previous one so it fuses with the surrounding system.
  6. ProtectThe patch receives the same protective finish as the rest of the roof.

If patches keep multiplying and new leaks appear every season, it is time to replace the waterproofing rather than patch it again.

Sloping surfaces and concrete pitched roofs

On sloping surfaces such as concrete pitched roofs, ramps and steep roof sections, two points need particular attention. First, the bitumen must have a high softening point so it does not creep down the slope in summer; with an unsuitable grade, a few hot seasons are enough for the bitumen to thin at the top of the slope and pile up at the bottom. Second, the jute strips must be laid so that the laps follow the flow of water, with each upper strip overlapping the one below. On steep slopes, a mechanical protection layer or a final covering such as sheeting or tiles over the waterproofing noticeably increases its life. In hot climates, a reflective finish on any exposed sloping section is strongly recommended.

What makes a bitumen roof last longer or fail sooner?

Longer life

  • The right bitumen grade, heated to the right temperature.
  • Two fully saturated layers of jute with offset laps.
  • Correct falls and no ponding.
  • A protective layer: tiles, asphalt or reflective coating.
  • Annual inspection and clean drains.

Shorter life

  • Direct sun without protection.
  • Burnt bitumen or a grade with a low softening point.
  • A damp substrate and blisters left in place.
  • Foot traffic, sharp tools and heavy plant on the waterproofing.
  • Ponding and silt on the surface.

Eight questions to ask before appointing a contractor

  1. Which bitumen grade will you use, and will it be written into the contract?
  2. Is the jute new and dry, or second-hand? Can we see a sample?
  3. How many layers will you lay, and how will the laps of the second layer be positioned relative to the first?
  4. Are primer and fillets included in the price?
  5. How high will the system go up the walls, and how will the top edge be protected?
  6. How will drains and pipes be detailed? Will they get reinforcing layers?
  7. Will you carry out a flood test before the protective layer or tiles?
  8. Is your warranty in writing, and exactly what does it cover?

A contractor who answers these questions clearly and in writing is usually the one who also does the work properly.

Documentation on consultant-supervised projects

On projects managed by a main contractor or a consultant, good workmanship must also be documented. A waterproofing subcontractor is normally asked for:

  • Material submittals: technical data sheets for the bitumen, primer and reinforcement, manufacturers' certificates and samples on request.
  • Method statement: the sequence from preparation to testing, the equipment, and safety measures, particularly for hot works.
  • Detail drawings: parapet, drain, penetration, expansion joint and edge details approved by the consultant.
  • Inspection and test plan: hold points at which the supervisor inspects before the next stage, such as the substrate before priming, the falls, and the flood test.
  • Hot work permit: where the site requires one before the boiler is lit.
  • Daily records and photographs: especially of work that will later be buried.
  • Flood test record and warranty certificate: at handover.

We prepare these documents in English and Arabic and follow the hold points set by the consultant. Well-documented work saves supervisors time and makes handover and interim payments straightforward.

Weather and season

  • Do not work in rain, fog or on a wet surface.
  • Severe cold cools the bitumen before the jute is laid and weakens the bond between layers; work in the warmest hours.
  • In extreme heat, as in the Gulf and southern Iraq in summer, work is easier and safer in the early morning and late afternoon, and traffic on fresh work must be limited.
  • High winds and dust contaminate the hot bitumen, lift the fabric and increase the risk of hot bitumen splashing.

Safety with hot bitumen

Hot bitumen is one of the most dangerous materials on a construction site. Burns from it are deep and painful because it sticks to skin and keeps transferring heat. Crews and site managers should enforce:

  • Heat-resistant gloves, safety boots, long sleeves and eye protection for everyone handling hot bitumen.
  • A stable boiler away from combustibles, never overfilled or overheated.
  • Never pour water onto hot bitumen or into the boiler; the water flashes to steam and throws hot bitumen out.
  • A suitable fire extinguisher by the boiler and on the roof.
  • Clear routes for carrying hot bitumen, and sound buckets with secure handles.
  • Fall protection at unprotected edges.
  • Adequate ventilation in enclosed spaces such as basements and bathrooms, where fumes build up.

What affects the cost?

  • Bitumen grade and its current price, and the fabric type.
  • Number of layers, and whether critical points get reinforcing layers.
  • Preparation: stripping old waterproofing, correcting falls and forming fillets.
  • Details: length of parapets, number of drains and pipes.
  • Access: building height and where the boiler can be placed.
  • Protective layer: sand, reflective coating or tiles.

When comparing quotations, make sure all of them price the same scope: number of layers, bitumen grade, upstand height, primer, flood test and protective layer.

Frequently asked questions

Is one layer of bitumen and jute enough?

For roofs and foundations, a two-layer system is recommended. A single layer is only suitable for minor or temporary work and does not give adequate security against cracking and leaks.

Which bitumen is used for roof waterproofing?

For roofs exposed to the sun, an oxidised grade such as 85/25 or 90/15 is usually used because it softens at a higher temperature. Under floor finishes and in foundations, 60/70 is sometimes used according to the specification.

Is bitumen and jute better than an Isogam membrane?

Each has its place. Membranes are faster and more uniform on exposed roofs; bitumen and jute excels under tiles, in foundations, in wet areas and on surfaces with many details.

Why does bitumen roofing crack after a while?

The most common cause is leaving it exposed to the sun without protection. Burnt bitumen, an unsuitable grade and ponding water also accelerate cracking.

Can a membrane be laid over an old bitumen and jute roof?

If the old system is sound, dry and fully bonded, usually yes. A rotten or blistered system must be removed first.

How high should the system go up the parapet?

At least 200–300 mm, with the top edge tucked into a chase or protected so water cannot get behind it.

How long does the work take?

It depends on the area, the number of details and the preparation needed. A typical residential roof takes several working days, plus drying time for screeds and primer and the flood test.

Can bitumen roofing be painted?

Yes, with coatings designed for bitumen, such as aluminium roof paint. Ordinary paints may react with bitumen, stain or crack.

Conclusion

Hot bitumen and jute roofing looks simple, but its quality lies in details that disappear once the work is finished: a dry substrate, primer, bitumen temperature, fully saturated fabric, laps and offset layers, upstands, drain details, a flood test and a protective layer. When these are respected, the system is a durable and reliable waterproofing, particularly under tiles, in foundations and in wet areas.
